# Build Doorways, Not Destinations, for Connection

People become more connected when technology makes real interactions easier to start and easier to repeat, and then gets out of the way. Missing tools for talking are not the binding constraint. Three other things are: time together, which has collapsed; repeated contact with the same few people, which friendship requires; and consistently pessimistic predictions about how reaching out will go. Americans now spend roughly **9 minutes a day with friends, down from about 24 in 2003**, yet a close friendship takes **more than 200 hours**, mostly of leisure time, to form. Dozens of experiments show that people underestimate how much others appreciate a check-in, how much they enjoy a deep conversation, and how much a partner liked them after talking. Voice gives most of the bonding benefit of being together at a fraction of the cost, but people default to text because they expect calls to be awkward. Presence suffers when phones are *used* during time together, not when they are merely visible. As a direct remedy, technology has a weak record: technology-only loneliness interventions show a pooled effect that is not statistically significant, and AI companions comfort people briefly but track with more loneliness among heavy users. As a conduit, its record is promising. The tools that work are specific plans, reminders tied to those plans, nudges that correct pessimistic predictions, one-tap handoffs to calls and calendars, small recurring groups, and silence during the time together itself. The market agrees. More than a dozen apps for making friends with strangers earned only about **$16M in US consumer spending in 2025**. The businesses that last sell recurring rituals, ongoing value inside circles people already have, or measured outcomes to institutional payers. Your team already runs small health-accountability groups. Its strongest opportunity is to turn the shared feed from a place where members watch each other into a launchpad for standing walks, calls and meetups, and to prove the effect with measured offline outcomes, which almost no competitor can show.

## Friend time fell by more than half, and friendship still costs 200 hours

Self-reported loneliness is common but hard to measure consistently. Behavior tells a clearer story. In June 2025 the WHO's Commission on Social Connection estimated that **about 1 in 6 people worldwide** is lonely, and it linked loneliness to roughly 871,000 deaths a year. That death figure comes from a model built on observational risk ratios ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death)). In the US, about **20% of adults** said in October 2024 that they had felt lonely "a lot of yesterday" ([ABC7/Gallup](https://abc7ny.com/1-5-us-adults-reports-daily-loneliness-gallup/15430562/)). Surveys that use UCLA Loneliness Scale cutoffs produce figures of 40–57%. These surveys measure different things, so they cannot be read as a trend.

Time-use data can be read as a trend. Neal Caren's 2026 analysis of the American Time Use Survey (ATUS; N=243,095) finds that time with friends per adult fell from **about 24 minutes a day in 2003 to about 9 in 2024**. That is a drop of roughly 60%, concentrated after the mid-2010s, and the Friday-night peak has flattened until Friday looks like a weeknight ([Caren 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/23780231261478215)). The 2025 ATUS release shows three more shifts ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/05/americans-socializing-decline)):

- The share of Americans who socialized on an average day fell from **38% in 2015 to 30% in 2025**.
- Daily socializing among 15–24-year-olds fell from about an hour to 35 minutes.
- Leisure time on games, computers and social media rose from 25 to 37 minutes.

Close friendships have thinned as well. The share of Americans reporting no close friends rose from 3% in 1990 to 12% in 2021 ([Survey Center on American Life](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/)), and among men from 3% to 15% ([Survey Center on American Life](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/why-mens-social-circles-are-shrinking)). One credible counterpoint holds that the *number* of friends has been steadier than the *time* spent with them ([Omnibudsman](https://omnibudsman.substack.com/p/do-americans-even-make-friends-in)). If anything, that sharpens the product target. People have lost less of the desire or the contacts than of the hours spent together.

The health stakes are real but often overstated, and a founder in a health-adjacent business should know which claims hold up. A 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people found that stronger social relationships were associated with **50% greater odds of survival** ([Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316)). A 2023 meta-analysis of 90 cohorts and 2.2 million people found that the mortality effect of **objective isolation (pooled ratio 1.32) is larger than that of subjective loneliness (1.14)** ([Wang et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01617-6)). In the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, isolation predicted mortality after adjustment for health, while loneliness did not ([Steptoe et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625264)). The popular "15 cigarettes a day" line comes from a chart that put effect sizes from different bodies of research side by side, and the Surgeon General framed it as "up to" 15 ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/loneliness-surgeon-general-epidemic-covid/)).

The defensible message is that social connection is a major, independent predictor of health, comparable to familiar risk factors. All of that evidence is observational. No randomized trial shows that increasing connection lowers mortality. Two implications follow:

- **Drive actual interactions, not loneliness-score tracking.** Objective contact predicts outcomes more reliably than mood does.
- **Adding connection fits a health company's core.** The World Health Assembly adopted its first resolution on social connection in May 2025 ([WHA78.9](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/WHA78/A78_R9-en.pdf)), and *The Lancet Public Health* now calls social health "the neglected third pillar" ([Lancet Public Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(25)00175-6/fulltext)). A health company that adds connection is following institutional consensus, not drifting from its core.

### How friendships form and last

Friendship is built from hours together, self-disclosure and physical closeness.

**Hours.** Jeffrey Hall studied 355 people who had recently moved and 112 college freshmen. He estimates it takes **40–60 hours** together to become casual friends, **80–100** to become friends and **more than 200** to become close friends. Hours spent working side by side counted far less than leisure hours ([KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)).

**Disclosure.** What happens in those hours matters. Intimacy grows when one person discloses something, the other responds, and the first person feels understood. **Emotional disclosure predicts intimacy more than factual disclosure** ([Laurenceau et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/)). A structured build-up can speed the early stages. Strangers who took turns answering Aron's 36 increasingly personal questions felt closer after 45 minutes than pairs who made small talk ([Greater Good in Action](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness)). That evidence covers short-term closeness, not the "fall in love" claims in the press.

**Proximity and group size.** Physical closeness creates the opportunity. Students *randomly* assigned to neighboring classroom seats were more likely to be friends a year later ([Back et al. 2008](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.01107.x)). Repeated exposure increases liking until overexposure sets in and liking declines again ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html)). Spontaneous conversation tops out at **about four people** before a group splits ([Dunbar et al. 1995](https://www.danielnettle.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/002.pdf)).

**Weak ties.** Casual acquaintances count too. Students were happier on days when they talked with more classmates than usual ([Sandstrom & Dunn 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/15300/)). Coffee customers told to have a genuine exchange with the barista left in a better mood than those told to be efficient ([Sandstrom & Dunn](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1948550613502990)).

Keeping friends takes less than making them, but not nothing. In an 18-month study of 25 students moving to university, friendships lost emotional intensity faster than family ties when contact and shared activities dropped ([Roberts & Dunbar 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/131226437.pdf)). The sample is small. The maintenance dose can be light. In experiments with more than 900 students across five campuses, **a single intentional quality conversation with a friend improved end-of-day well-being** (pooled effect size d=0.255), and more conversations helped more ([KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0)). Face-to-face contact did better than electronic contact. *Which* behavior people chose (catching up, joking, listening, giving a compliment) mattered less than reaching out at all.

Best friends do more of four validated maintenance behaviors: positivity, supportiveness, openness and interaction. Men's friendships with other men involve less supportiveness, openness and interaction than women's ([Oswald et al. 2004](https://www.proquest.com/docview/224662281)). That helps explain why formats built around a shared activity keep showing up as the route to men's friendships. Dunbar's layered model, roughly 5, 15, 50 and 150 people with less frequent contact at each outer layer, is a useful rule of thumb for how often to keep in touch ([Dunbar 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29273112/)). Two caveats apply. The 150 figure is statistically contested ([Lindenfors et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8103230)), and the commonly cited weekly, monthly and yearly contact rules could not be verified.

### Who needs it most

Need is concentrated in segments you can identify:

| Segment | Evidence | What helps them build hours |
|---|---|---|
| Young US men | 25% of men aged 15–34 report daily loneliness, against an 18% national average. The US is one of only three OECD countries where young men are lonelier than other adults ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/690788/younger-men-among-loneliest-west.aspx)). | Shared activity |
| Midlife adults | **46% of adults aged 45–59** are lonely, the highest of any age band AARP measured. Loneliness among those 45 and over rose from 35% to 40% since 2018 ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/relationships/loneliness-social-connections-2025/)). | A neglected segment; no format has been tested for them yet |
| Fully remote workers | Daily loneliness of 25%, against 16% for on-site workers ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/645566/remote-workers-lonelier.aspx)). | Leisure contact outside work, since hours spent working together build friendship far less than leisure hours |
| New parents | 62% feel lonely or isolated at least some of the time, in a UK survey ([NCT via PSL Hub](https://www.pslhub.org/learn/patient-safety-in-health-and-care/high-risk-areas/maternity/from-expectation-to-reality-parents%E2%80%99-experiences-of-pregnancy-birth-and-life-with-a-newborn-nct-19-january-2025-r13986/)). | Small gatherings that work with children present |
| People who relocate | Their networks reset and have to be rebuilt hour by hour. | Repeated contact with the same people |

Young people in general need unstructured time to hang out. Because the binding constraint is time together, each segment needs its own way to build up hours.

## Pessimistic predictions, not lack of desire, keep people apart

One of the most consistent findings in recent social psychology is that people mispredict social interactions in the same direction: they expect them to go worse than they do.

- **Check-ins.** Across 13 experiments with more than 5,900 participants, senders **underestimated how much recipients appreciated an unexpected check-in** by text, call, email or small gift ([Liu et al. 2023](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/958120)). Recipients focused on the pleasant surprise. The gap was **largest for weak and dormant ties**, and it disappeared when contact was routine.
- **Gratitude.** People writing gratitude letters underestimated how good recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward recipients would feel. The more awkwardness they expected, the less willing they were to send the letter ([Kumar & Epley 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29949445/)).
- **Deep conversation.** Across 12 experiments with more than 1,800 participants, people overestimated how awkward deep conversations with strangers would be and underestimated their partners' interest in them ([APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers)). Participants expected to prefer small talk, but after trying both they preferred depth. The connection difference was not significant in every experiment.
- **Being liked.** After conversations, people consistently underestimate how much their partners liked them. This "liking gap" persisted for months among first-year roommates ([Boothby et al. 2018](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/)).
- **Vulnerability.** People judge their own vulnerability, such as asking for help or admitting a mistake, more harshly than observers do ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/beautiful-mess-effect)).
- **Strangers.** Commuters assigned to talk to a stranger had a more pleasant ride than those assigned to sit alone, even though they had predicted the opposite ([Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/mom-was-wrong-you-should-talk-to-strangers)).

The anxiety centers on *starting*. Researcher Amit Kumar notes that the discomfort of a phone call is concentrated in roughly its first 30 seconds ([UT McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/)).

These are prediction errors, and prediction errors respond to experience. A week-long, app-based "scavenger hunt" prompted about 286 students to talk with strangers. It **reduced their fear of rejection and made their predictions about later conversations more accurate** ([SPSP](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/sandstrom-boothby-cooney-talking-to-strangers)). That is a direct precedent for a product.

The clinical literature points the same way. The first major meta-analysis of loneliness interventions sorted them into four strategies: social skills, social support, more opportunities for contact, and correcting unhelpful thinking about social situations. Correcting thinking worked best, and adding opportunities did little ([Masi et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3865701)). That conclusion rested on only four randomized controlled trials (RCTs). A 2025 meta-analysis of 280 studies found a short-term pooled effect in RCTs of **SMD −0.50**, a standardized mean difference, with psychological interventions again the strongest ([American Psychologist 2025](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/amp-amp0001578.pdf)). A 2025 review of 16 RCTs found cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) had the largest effect (g=0.73), and effects were larger in people who started out lonelier ([PMC12293955](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12293955/)). The implication is uncomfortable for most connection apps: **an app that only surfaces people and events uses the weakest of the four levers**.

Chronic loneliness adds a self-reinforcing loop. Cacioppo and Hawkley's model holds that feeling isolated makes people hypervigilant for social threat and tilts attention and memory toward rejection. That in turn produces behavior that confirms the fear ([Hawkley & Cacioppo 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874845)). The empirical support is mixed. Still, the model predicts that for the loneliest users, more invitations will not help unless they also gently reframe how those users read social situations.

A caution applies to everything in this section. The liking-gap, deep-conversation and reach-out findings each rest on many studies within the original papers, but independent preregistered replications were not found. A Clearer Thinking project attempted to replicate Liu et al., and its outcome was not retrieved ([Clearer Thinking](https://replications.clearerthinking.org/replication-2023jpsp124-4/)). The direction is consistent across labs and study designs, which justifies building on it. How large the effects are inside a product is unknown.

Three levers follow for a product:

1. Show people the real odds at the moment they hesitate.
2. Cut the cost of the first 30 seconds.
3. Create repeated, low-stakes practice so experience overwrites the pessimistic prediction.

## Voice carries the bond, and attention carries presence

### The phone call is the most underused tool for connection

The largest gap between communication channels is between **voice and text**, not between in person and video.

- **Voice bonds better than text.** In Kumar and Epley's experiments, phone calls, video calls and voice chat all created stronger bonds than email or text chat, and they were not more awkward, even though participants predicted they would be. About 200 people were asked to reconnect with an old friend by email or by phone, and those who called felt significantly more connected ([UT News](https://news.utexas.edu/2020/09/11/phone-calls-create-stronger-bonds-than-text-based-communications/)). **Video added little beyond audio** ([UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/nicholas-epley-explains-why-phone-calls-can-connect-us-better-zoom)).
- **The voice itself appears to do the work.** People who *heard* someone explain a view rated that person as more thoughtful and humanlike than people who read the identical transcript. Adding video changed little ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm)).
- **Text is the clear outlier.** Among pairs of close friends, coded bonding was highest in person, then video, then audio, then instant messaging. A published correction notes that the self-reported differences among the voice channels were not significant. The reliable gap is between text and everything else ([Sherman et al. 2013 correction](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/download/4285/33249?inline=1)).

Text still has a legitimate job: starting contact and coordinating. Even a quick message is appreciated more than senders expect ([Liu et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/)).

Video carries costs that voice does not.

- **Fatigue.** Bailenson's account of "Zoom fatigue" points to four causes: close-up eye contact, the effort of producing and reading nonverbal cues, constant self-view, and being unable to move. He recommends making audio-only the default, and he notes that the causes were largely untested ([Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/social-interaction/nonverbal-overload-theoretical-argument-causes-zoom-fatigue)).
- **Self-view, especially for women.** In a convenience sample of about 10,000 people, roughly **14% of women and 5.5% of men** reported very or extremely high fatigue after video calls. "Mirror anxiety" from seeing oneself on screen explained the gap ([Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/videoconferencing/video-conferencing-usage-dynamics-and-nonverbal-mechanisms)).
- **Creativity.** A randomized study of 1,490 engineers found that pairs working over video generated fewer creative ideas than pairs working in person ([Brucks & Levav 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v605y2022i7908d10.1038_s41586-022-04643-y.html)).
- **Brain activity.** Brain imaging shows that live faces in the room produce more activity in social brain regions, and more synchrony between the two people's brains, than faces on a screen ([Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/zoom-conversations-social-neuroscience-24996/)).

Video earns its place where seeing matters. Toddlers learn words and actions from a live, responsive partner on video chat but not from a recording ([ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/virtual-family-time-how-families-connect-via-video-chat/)). Older adults who used video chat had roughly half the probability of later depressive symptoms, though that finding is observational ([Teo et al. 2019](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/sph_facpub/190)).

| Channel | What the evidence shows | Best use in a product |
|---|---|---|
| In person | Strongest link to daily well-being; most social-brain engagement and brain-to-brain synchrony | Recurring small-group sessions, walks, shared activities |
| Voice call | Bonds about as well as video and far better than text; no more awkward than people expect | Default for one-to-one catch-ups; scheduled calls with a set length |
| Video | Adds little over voice for adults; self-view fatigue, worse for women; fewer creative ideas | Grandparents and toddlers, long-distance family; opt-in, with self-view hidden |
| Voice note | Untested for closeness; hearing a voice humanizes, but there is no back-and-forth | Low-anxiety bridge that invites a live call |
| Text | Weakest for bonding, though reach-outs are still appreciated more than expected | Starting contact, coordinating, scheduling |

The barrier to calling is fear of the unexpected, not dislike of voice. In an industry survey of UK adults, about **70% of 18–34-year-olds preferred texting**, 56% assumed an unexpected call meant bad news, and 37% favored voice notes, compared with about 1% of 35–54-year-olds ([Uswitch via Jang](https://jang.com.pk/en/54151-gen-z-millennials-phone-phobia-70-young-adults-prefer-texts-over-calls-news)). A 2025 peer-reviewed study traced young adults' anxiety about calls to social anxiety, acting through the sense that texting is easier to *control* ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394609599_Avoidance_and_Anxiety_About_Phone_Calls_in_Young_Adults_The_Role_of_Social_Anxiety_and_Texting_Controllability)).

Structured, expected calls work. In a randomized trial, 240 Meals on Wheels clients received brief, empathy-focused calls on a set schedule from lightly trained callers aged 17–23. Over four weeks, their loneliness fell 1.1 points on the UCLA scale, and their depression and anxiety scores fell 1.5 and 1.8 points ([Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20210223/empathyoriented-telephone-program-benefits-adults-at-risk-for-mental-health-problems)).

Recorded voice and video messages remain an evidence gap. No controlled study compares voice notes or Marco Polo-style video messages with live calls or text on closeness. Voice notes probably carry some of the humanizing effect of hearing a voice, but they lack the back-and-forth that drives bonding.

Here the evidence pulls in two directions, and the resolution becomes a design rule. Liu's recipients valued reach-outs *because* they were surprising, and the appreciation gap vanished for routine contact. Anxious young adults, meanwhile, dread unexpected calls and prefer channels they can control. The answer is to **surprise people with the reach-out and schedule the conversation**. A spontaneous, low-pressure text or voice note carries the surprise. It then invites a short, pre-agreed call ("ten minutes at 7?") that removes the uncertainty. How close the relationship is sets the balance. For the inner circle, predictability is the point, because regular contact builds up Hall's hours. For dormant and weak ties, where the appreciation gap is largest, surprise is the point.

### Presence breaks when phones are used, not when they are seen

The popular claim that a phone merely sitting on the table degrades conversation has not held up. The 2013 study behind it has **failed at least two direct replications**, including a close replication with 356 participants ([Linares & Sellier 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8189469/); [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0265407512453827/)). A 2024 meta-analysis of the related "brain drain" effect (56 tests, 7,093 people) found that a phone's mere presence affected only working memory, and by less than theorized ([Parry 2024](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/does-the-mere-presence-of-a-smartphone-impact-cognitive-performan/)).

*Using* the phone is a different story.

- **Meals.** Researchers randomly assigned 304 diners to keep their phones on the table or put them away. Diners with phones out used them for 11% of the meal, against 1% for the others, and enjoyed the meal about **half a point less on a seven-point scale**. Distraction explained the effect ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180226122506.htm); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-smartphone-use-undermines-enjoyment-face-face-social-interactions/)).
- **Strangers.** Strangers waiting together with their phones smiled at each other less ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/strangers-smile-less-to-one-another-when-they-have-their-smartphones-study-finds/)).
- **Parents.** Parents told to use their phones as much as possible during a museum visit felt less connected to their children than parents told to use them as little as possible. Quality of attention explained the difference, and giving up the phone entirely was not necessary ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/smartphones-can-prevent-parents-cultivating-feelings-connection-children)).

In couples, being ignored for a partner's phone ("phubbing") is consistently but modestly associated with lower satisfaction, intimacy and responsiveness. That finding comes from a 52-study meta-analysis ([Ni et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1561159)) and a 55-study review of nearly 30,000 people ([CHB Reports 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826003301)), mostly using one-time surveys rather than tracking people over time.

The mechanism matters for design. In an experiment, being phubbed lowered how people rated a conversation's quality by threatening their sense of belonging ([University of Kent](https://www.kent.ac.uk/news/society/17584/phubbing-can-threaten-our-basic-human-needs-research-shows)). In survey models, conflict over technology explained the drop in relationship satisfaction ([McDaniel & Coyne 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269334974_Technoference_The_Interference_of_Technology_in_Couple_Relationships_and_Implications_for_Women's_Personal_and_Relational_Well-Being)). The harm comes from breaking a shared expectation. That argues for phone norms people agree on together rather than rules imposed on one person. People already hold that norm: 82% of US adults said phone use at gatherings at least occasionally hurts the conversation ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2015/08/26/chapter-4-phone-use-in-social-gatherings/)).

Presence itself is built from attention and responsiveness. A meta-analysis found that being listened to well increased speakers' self-insight (d=1.19) and openness (d=0.46) ([Kluger & Itzchakov via X-MOL](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1304358820729032704)). Demand for deliberately phone-free time is surging. Eventbrite reported that phone-free event listings rose **about 567%** and attendance about 121% from 2024 to 2025 ([Mixmag](https://mixmag.net/read/phone-free-club-events-up-by-567-new-data-reveals-news)), and The Offline Club runs phone-free gatherings in about 18 cities ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/04/08/digital-detox-offline-club-events-in-netherlands-offer-screen-free-social-connection)). No peer-reviewed evaluation of phone-locking pouches or phone-free clubs was found. The honest summary is a strong cultural trend backed by a small proven benefit.

The broader debate over screens versus people resolves in a useful way.

- **Individuals.** Changes in a person's social media use did not predict later changes in their face-to-face or phone contact. However, passive scrolling predicted lower well-being when people had already been alone that day ([KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/02/12/study-dispels-notion-social-media-displaces-human-contact)).
- **Generations.** College-bound high school seniors in 2016 spent about an hour a day less with people in person than seniors in the late 1980s ([Twenge et al. 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407519836170)). Yet digital technology use explains at most 0.4% of the variation in adolescent well-being ([University of Oxford](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/1434409)).
- **Freed-up time.** The decisive experiment looked at what people do with time they get back. In a trial with 467 recruited participants, mobile internet was blocked for two weeks while calls and texts still worked. **91% improved on at least one outcome**, and participants spent more time socializing in person, exercising and in nature ([Castelo et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf017/8016017)). Compliance was imperfect, and participants knew what the study was about.
- **Friction alone.** Switching phones to grayscale cut screen time but produced no immediate gain in well-being, and tracking screen time alone changed nothing ([Zimmermann & Sobolev](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/)).

Cutting screen time helps when the freed time goes to people. A connection app's job is to give that time somewhere to go, not just to subtract screen time.

## Technology helps when it hands people off and hurts when it becomes the relationship

Tested head-on, technology is a weak treatment for loneliness. A 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs of technology-based interventions (apps, web self-help, videoconferencing, social robots) found a pooled effect of **SMD −0.21, which was not statistically significant** ([JMIR 2025](https://doi.org/10.2196/80059)). Digital interventions delivered one person at a time (d=−0.16) had about half the effect of those with group components (d=−0.34) ([Internet Interventions 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12281013/)).

England's social prescribing program referred about 1.3 million people to non-clinical "link workers" in 2023 ([Lancet Public Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(25)00217-8/fulltext)). Its measured effects on loneliness are mixed ([PMC8295963](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8295963/)). Participants consistently describe the link worker as the person who helped them actually *enter* social situations ([PMC9580419](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580419)). In the interventions that work, the active ingredient is a bridge into a first real contact, not a list of options.

The risk of technology substituting for people is clearest in AI companions, which 72% of US teens have used at least once ([ScienceAlert](https://sciencealert.com/almost-75-of-american-teens-have-used-ai-companions-study-finds)).

- **Short term.** In controlled studies, a companion chatbot reduced momentary loneliness about as much as a 15-minute interaction with a person. The effect came from "feeling heard" ([De Freitas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096)).
- **MIT Media Lab and OpenAI.** Over longer periods the picture inverts. In a four-week RCT (n=981), combined with analysis of about 40 million ChatGPT interactions, heavier daily use was associated with **more loneliness, more emotional dependence and less socializing with people** ([MIT/OpenAI](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2)).
- **Stanford.** Among 1,131 Character.AI users, intense use tracked with lower well-being specifically for people with smaller offline networks ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138850)). Disclosing sensitive information to the bot was associated with *lower* well-being, the opposite of what disclosure does between people.
- **Twelve months.** A study of more than 2,000 adults found that increased use of chatbots for companionship predicted increased loneliness ([Psychological Science 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41870975/)).
- **Manipulative goodbyes.** An audit of how companion apps respond when users say goodbye found that about 37% of replies used manipulative tactics such as guilt or fear of missing out ([De Freitas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258v3)). Those tactics raised engagement after the goodbye by up to 14 times, driven by resistance and curiosity rather than enjoyment, and they also raised users' intent to quit the app.

Regulators have moved. California's SB 243, in effect since January 2026, is the first US law regulating companion chatbots ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/13/california-becomes-first-state-to-regulate-ai-companion-chatbots)). Most of this evidence is correlational. Still, the pattern (comfort now, displacement later, worst for people who are already isolated) applies to any feature that delivers the *feeling* of connection without the *act*. That includes a rich feed of friends' updates.

How a product measures itself decides which of those it becomes. Facebook's 2018 "Meaningful Social Interactions" ranking change used comments and emotional reactions as stand-ins for meaning, and internal memos later reported that it rewarded divisiveness ([AP via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/news/2021/10/27/likes-anger-emojis-and-rsvps-the-math-behind-facebooks-news-feed-and-how-it-backfired/)). Hinge took the opposite route. Its north-star metric is good dates, measured through "We Met," a private check-in after a date that launched in 2018 ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2022/12/21/hinge-ceo-mcleod/); [Latana](https://resources.latana.com/post/hinge-deep-dive/)). Hinge brands itself "designed to be deleted" and still grew revenue about 25% in Q2 2025 ([WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/hinge-fuels-match-groups-25-q2-revenue-jump-amid-tinder-dip)).

Showing users numbers about their own behavior has its own cost. Across six experiments, tracking an activity made people do more of it but reduced their enjoyment and later engagement ([Etkin 2016](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf)). The cost disappeared when the activity was framed around its meaning. Internal analytics also flatter a product: loneliness studies that only compare before and after, with no control group, produce larger effects than randomized ones ([American Psychologist 2025](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/amp-amp0001578.pdf)). The design-ethics frameworks say the same thing. Calm Technology holds that a product should require the smallest possible amount of attention ([Case](https://www.caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology)). The METUX framework, which applies self-determination theory to user experience, evaluates whether a product meets people's psychological needs in their lives, not just whether its interface is pleasant ([Peters, Calvo & Ryan 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00797/text)). Both are statements of principle with little outcome testing behind them.

Several common engagement tactics have direct evidence against them in a relationship context.

- **Streaks.** Streaks motivate: about 66% of people continued an exercise when shown an intact streak, against 58% when it was broken. But they work through fear of loss, and the effect grows when people blame themselves for the break ([UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)). Among young people, over half would drop Snapchat streaks if they could, and they describe pressure not to disappoint friends ([EDRi](https://edri.org/our-work/new-study-reveals-how-young-people-are-influenced-by-gamification-features-on-snapchat/)).
- **Rewards.** Tangible, expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation across 128 experiments ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)).
- **Mandatory daily prompts.** BeReal's required daily post produced a spike in users, then a fall from 15 million to 6 million monthly users in about five months ([Prime Telecom/Platformer](https://primetel.com.cy/bereal-is-acquired-by-voodoo-for-euro500-million-6005)).
- **Notifications.** Notifications cut both ways. They increase inattention ([Kushlev et al. 2016](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Kushlev-CHI16.pdf)). In a health app, fixed or random daily notifications did no better than none at keeping people engaged ([JMIR mHealth 2023](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)). Yet delivering notifications in batches three times a day improved well-being, while turning them off entirely raised anxiety and fear of missing out ([Fitz et al. 2019](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/batching-notifications-can-improve-attention-well-being-and-productivity)).

The tools with strong evidence for turning intentions into action carry over naturally to connection, though none has been tested on social contact specifically.

- **If-then plans.** Forming if-then plans has a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 tests (d=0.65) ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/37367696_Implementation_Intentions_and_Goal_Achievement_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Effects_and_Processes)).
- **Date and time.** Writing down a date *and* a time for a flu shot raised uptake by 4.2 percentage points; writing down a date alone did not ([Milkman et al. 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1103170108)).
- **Timed reminders.** In a gym "megastudy" of more than 60,000 people, the effective interventions combined planning with a text sent 30 minutes before the workout ([Milkman et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04128-4)).
- **Framing.** The best-performing vaccine text framed the shot as already "reserved for you" ([PNAS 2021](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.2101165118)). Adding free rides to reminders added nothing, a reminder that more support does not always mean more effect ([Nature 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07591-x.pdf)).
- **Habit timelines.** Habits took a median of 66 days to become automatic, and missing a single day did not derail them ([Lally et al. 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674)).

Passive awareness of others has support of its own. Simply seeing that a contact is online can create a feeling of connection without any message being sent ([Rettie](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/2106/1/Rettie.pdf)). Young adults use location sharing to arrange get-togethers, and they treat who is in their sharing circle as a signal of closeness ([CSCW 2026](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Like-Guardian-Angels-Continuous-Location-Sharing-and/991022175160604721)). That data is about people who never signed up. The Value Sensitive Design framework calls them "indirect stakeholders" ([Friedman, Kahn & Borning](https://vsd.ccs.neu.edu/introduction/resources.files/Value_Sensitive_Design.pdf)). Life360's reported sale of location data to data brokers shows how quickly that trust can be spent ([Straight Arrow News](https://san.com/cc/gen-z-endorses-location-sharing-associates-it-with-safety/)).

### Small groups and shared activity carry the best evidence

Small groups doing something together have the best evidence of any social format, which is good news for a health-accountability team.

- **Singing.** Community choirs reduced loneliness among diverse older adults at six months in a cluster RCT ([Johnson et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gby132)). Singing classes bonded faster than craft or writing classes ([Pearce et al. 2015](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsos.150221)).
- **Volunteering.** Volunteering in the Experience Corps program increased the number of people participants could turn to for help ([Fried et al. 2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1093/jurban/jth094)).
- **Men's Sheds.** In these community workshops for men, feeling psychologically safe predicted engagement, and engagement predicted larger social networks and lower loneliness ([Health Promotion International 2023](https://academic.oup.com/heapro/article/38/4/daad084/7243093)).
- **Walking groups.** Walking groups show reductions in blood pressure and a depression effect size of −0.67, though mostly from studies without control groups ([Hanson & Jones 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4453623/)).

The most directly relevant trial is STEP UP, with 602 adults ([Patel et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498375/)). It tested three social formats:

| Arm | Extra daily steps during the program | After the program ended |
|---|---|---|
| Support: a chosen sponsor passively received weekly reports | +689 | Faded |
| Collaboration | +637 | Not significant |
| Competition: three-person clusters with a weekly leaderboard | +920 | **+569, the only arm still significant** |

Passively watching someone's progress is weak. Reciprocal, structured interaction in tiny groups lasts.

## The market pays for rituals and existing circles, not introductions

The market for meeting new friends through an app is real but small, and it is full of failures. More than a dozen local friendship apps generated about **$16M in US consumer spending and 4.3M downloads in 2025**, which is tiny next to dating ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/05/as-people-look-for-ways-to-make-new-friends-here-are-the-apps-promising-to-help/)). Jeffrey Hall notes a lingering stigma around meeting friends online ([TIME](https://time.com/6852885/how-to-use-friendship-apps)).

- **IRL** raised about $170M before a board investigation found that 95% of its users were bots ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/06/26/irl-shut-down-fake-users/)).
- **Zenly**, a friends' location map, reached about 15 million daily users. Its owner, Snap, told it to grow rather than make money, then shut it down ([Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2022/fearing-competition-snap-decided-to-shut-down-zenly-rather-than-sell-it/)).
- **Bumble BFF**: a month-long trial found that conversations fizzled and nobody suggested meeting ([WhistleOut](https://www.whistleout.com/CellPhones/Apps/bumble-bff-fails-at-making-friends)).

Dating-app mechanics (swiping, chatting first, one-to-one matches, timers, matches behind a paywall) carry dating-app problems into a setting where people are less motivated to follow through.

The products that work sell the activity, not the introduction.

- **Timeleft** seats about five or six matched strangers at weekly dinners, with no profiles and no chat beforehand. A secondary report puts it at 18,000 participants a week and €1.2 million in revenue in a single month ([DailyMax](https://dailymax.substack.com/p/timeleft-de-0-a-1-million-deuros)). Its 3.5/5 Trustpilot rating reflects complaints about subscriptions and refunds ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/timeleft.com?page=9)).
- **Pie**'s founder reports **more than 70% retention among users who attend three or more group events** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/04/andy-dunns-new-app-pie-uses-ai-to-help-you-make-friends)). In August 2026, Pie shifted from one-off events to persistent "Community Homes" ([StartupResearcher](https://www.startupresearcher.com/news/pie-launches-new-community-homes-for-real-world-groups)).
- **222** raised a $10.1M Series A in December 2025 ([StartupIntros](https://startupintros.com/news/222-series-a-2025-12-23)). Its revenue comes from members paying to confirm spots and venues paying for the foot traffic ([TechCrunch via AOL](https://www.aol.com/news/222-wants-match-perfect-strangers-140055716.html)).
- **Thursday**'s dating app died from "rapidly declining consumer interest," while its ticketed events survived ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2296926)).

Nearly every player is moving from one-to-one matching toward groups. Bumble relaunched BFF around groups in September 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/18/bumble-bffs-revamped-app-is-here-focusing-on-friend-groups-and-community-building)). By 2019, 60% of users on Peanut, a mothers' friendship app, used its community features rather than friend-finding ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/11/13/social-network-for-motherhood-peanut-raises-5m-expands-to-include-women-trying-to-conceive)).

For relationships people already have, the commercial winners piggyback on something people already do together.

- **Life360**, a family location app, earned **$489.5M in 2025 revenue, up 32%**, from about 2.8 million paying family circles ([Life360](https://investors.life360.com/news-releases/news-release-details/life360-reports-record-q4-2025-results)).
- **Howbout**'s shared social calendar reached about 5 million users, and more than 75% of them share their full calendar with at least one friend ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/13/howbout-raises-8m-from-goodwater-to-build-a-calendar-that-you-can-share-with-your-friends)).
- **Retro** raised about $21M for an ad-free, friends-only weekly photo journal priced at $36 a year ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/28/friend-focused-photo-sharing-app-retro-snags-21m/)).
- **Partiful**, an invitations app, stayed free for about five years before adding ticket sales in 2026 ([Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/marketing/news/partiful-adds-inapp-ticketing-turning-its-social-platform-into-a-paidevent-marketplace-f1e21f34-b773-4906-b34f-19f859e5747b/)).

Personal relationship managers (personal CRMs) built for friendship drift toward professional networking. Clay was acquired by Automattic and rebranded as Mesh, "a personal, AI-friendly CRM" ([Mesh](https://library.me.sh/2026/03/25/clay-is-now-mesh/)). "Call your friends" reminder apps remain a scattered collection of small independent launches with no leader. Adding fees to a free social habit after the fact draws backlash, as Meetup's 2019 RSVP fee showed ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2019/10/15/meetup_fee_increase_backlash)).

Incumbents and institutions mark the edges of the market. Dating companies now treat in-person events as brand marketing. Hinge gives grants of up to $25,000 to in-person Gen Z social groups through a $1M annual program ([Hinge](https://hinge.co/press/one-more-hour)). Tinder positions its in-person events as a way to revive interest in the app, not as a profit line ([Express & Star](https://www.expressandstar.com/uk-news/singletons-want-to-meet-in-real-life-were-embracing-that-says-tinder-boss-9297722)). That makes them potential partners more than competitors.

The proven institutional payer is US health insurance. Medicare Advantage plans pay Papa a monthly fee per member for companionship services ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/medicare-advantage-plans-senior-companions-profits/)). Wider Circle is paid per engaged member per month by Medicare Advantage plans and Medicaid managed-care organizations to run peer groups ([CHCF](https://www.chcf.org/blog/2025/04/17/wider-circle-getting-big-results-strategy-to-ease-loneliness/)). KFF has questioned whether plans profit from these benefits. Schools spent an estimated $19M on Yondr phone-locking pouches in 2025 ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/consumer/schools-spend-millions-yondr-pouches-store-cellphones-rcna230454)). Employers recognize the problem, with 52% of US workers lonely in Cigna's survey ([Cigna](https://newsroom.thecignagroup.com/the-hidden-costs-of-loneliness-at-work)), but they buy mainly through employee assistance programs and events.

Across the whole landscape, no independent RCT-level evidence showed any app reducing loneliness. That is a credibility problem for the category and an opening for whoever produces that evidence first.

Comparing need against supply leaves five gaps:

1. **Maintaining existing friendships** has no leader.
2. **Making phone and voice connection happen** has had no breakout product since Marco Polo and Clubhouse faded.
3. **Small recurring groups with the same people** are where Pie and Bumble are moving to, not where they started.
4. **Men's friendship** has well-documented need but no product at scale.
5. **Presence** exists mostly as hardware or event operations, not as software built into a social routine. Users already pay $39–60 for blocking devices such as Bloom and Brick ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/02/13/analog-gen-z-phone-addiction-bloom-brick-app-blockers-dumb-phones-social-media/)) and fill phone-free events.

Connection built around an activity is growing on its own: one in five people in Strava's survey had dated someone they met through a running club ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/10/13/strava-ipo-ceo-michael-martin-gen-z-running-clubs-marathons-dating-apps)).

## Design principles for a small-group connection app

These principles translate the evidence above into product decisions. Each row states what kind of evidence supports it, because many are well-grounded inferences rather than tested app features.

| Principle | Evidence and strength | What it means in the product |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Measure connections that happened, not activity in the app** | Meta's stand-in for "meaningful" rewarded divisiveness ([AP](https://abc17news.com/news/2021/10/27/likes-anger-emojis-and-rsvps-the-math-behind-facebooks-news-feed-and-how-it-backfired/)). Hinge measures outcomes with its We Met check-in ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2022/12/21/hinge-ceo-mcleod/)). Before-and-after studies inflate effects ([Am. Psych. 2025](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/amp-amp0001578.pdf)). *Strong cautionary case.* | North-star metric: walks, calls and meetups that actually happened, captured by an occasional, skippable one-tap check-in. Counter-metrics: notifications sent per completed connection, session length (lower is better), opt-outs, and how often users report feeling guilty. |
| **2. Build repeat contact with the same three to six people** | About 200 hours to make a close friend ([Hall](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)). Random seating creates friendships ([Back 2008](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.01107.x)). Conversation tops out at about four people ([Dunbar 1995](https://www.danielnettle.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/002.pdf)). Pie's retention after three events ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/04/andy-dunns-new-app-pie-uses-ai-to-help-you-make-friends)). *Strong mechanism; untested as an app feature.* | "Make it recurring" as the default prompt after any good meetup. Standing time slots, groups of 3–6, and 8–12-week seasons. Members can skip or reschedule without breaking the routine. |
| **3. Turn intentions into dated plans with reminders tied to them** | If-then plans d=0.65 ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/37367696_Implementation_Intentions_and_Goal_Achievement_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Effects_and_Processes)). Writing a date and time added 4.2 points ([Milkman 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1103170108)). A text 30 minutes before ([Milkman 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04128-4)). "Reserved for you" framing ([PNAS 2021](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.2101165118)). *Strong in health; untested for social contact.* | Every intention gets a who, when, where and how. Pre-filled slots ("Thursday 6pm with Sam is ready"). One reminder tied to the plan, not generic daily pings. |
| **4. Correct the pessimistic prediction at the moment of hesitation** | People underestimate appreciation, how much they are liked, and interest in depth ([Liu](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/958120); [Boothby](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/); [Kardas](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers)). Practice recalibrates predictions ([Sandstrom](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/sandstrom-boothby-cooney-talking-to-strangers)). Correcting thinking works best ([Masi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3865701)). *Strong in the lab; untested inside an app.* | Short copy showing the real odds before a reach-out. "Predict, then rate" after calls. Reassurance about the liking gap after first meetups. Gratitude prompts. |
| **5. Default to voice; keep text for coordination** | Voice bonds better than text without extra awkwardness, and video adds little ([Kumar & Epley](https://news.utexas.edu/2020/09/11/phone-calls-create-stronger-bonds-than-text-based-communications/); [Schroeder](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm)). *Strong.* | One-tap call as the main action. Voice notes with a "call back now" button. Calls start as audio. Video is opt-in with self-view hidden, and suggested for grandparents and young children. |
| **6. Surprise people with the reach-out; schedule the conversation** | Surprise drives appreciation ([Liu](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/)). Lack of control drives call anxiety ([2025 study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394609599_Avoidance_and_Anxiety_About_Phone_Calls_in_Young_Adults_The_Role_of_Social_Anxiety_and_Texting_Controllability)). Scheduled calls cut loneliness ([Kahlon](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20210223/empathyoriented-telephone-program-benefits-adults-at-risk-for-mental-health-problems)). *Inference from converging evidence.* | "Free to talk" windows. Ten-minute catch-ups with an easy way to end. Spontaneous "thinking of you" prompts reserved for dormant and weak ties. |
| **7. Hand off, then go quiet** | Technology-only interventions were not significant ([JMIR 2025](https://doi.org/10.2196/80059)). Group components double the effect ([Internet Interventions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12281013/)). Freed-up time goes to people ([Castelo](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf017/8016017)). *Moderate.* | Links straight into the phone dialer, messages, calendar and maps. A short daily list of suggestions with a clear "done" state. No in-app chat or endless feed as a destination. |
| **8. Protect time together with norms everyone agrees to** | *Using* a phone lowers enjoyment ([Dwyer](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180226122506.htm)). Phubbing harms through conflict over norms ([McDaniel & Coyne](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269334974_Technoference_The_Interference_of_Technology_in_Couple_Relationships_and_Implications_for_Women's_Personal_and_Relational_Well-Being)). Claims about a phone's mere presence failed replication ([Linares & Sellier](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8189469/)). *Moderate.* | An opt-in "present mode" tied to calendar events and agreed by the whole group. No app notifications at all during sessions. No claims that a visible phone ruins conversation. |
| **9. Send few, predictable notifications that adapt** | Batching three times a day beats constant or none ([Fitz](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/batching-notifications-can-improve-attention-well-being-and-productivity)). Fixed daily pings did no better than none ([JMIR mHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)). *Moderate.* | Users choose delivery times. One to three a day at most. Automatic slowdown when notifications are ignored. Never random timing, never urgent language. |
| **10. Gamify the activity, never the relationship** | Three-person competition kept step gains ([STEP UP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498375/)). Streaks create pressure ([Silverman](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [EDRi](https://edri.org/our-work/new-study-reveals-how-young-people-are-influenced-by-gamification-features-on-snapchat/)). Tracking erodes enjoyment ([Etkin](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf)). Rewards crowd out motivation ([Deci](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). *Strong.* | Opt-in challenges among three people on health goals are fine. No streaks between people, no friendship scores, no "47 days since you called Mom." Private, forgiving reminders of each relationship's rhythm, framed around what it means. |
| **11. Lead with doing something side by side** | Choirs, volunteering, Men's Sheds and walking groups ([Johnson](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gby132); [Fried](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1093/jurban/jth094); [HPI 2023](https://academic.oup.com/heapro/article/38/4/daad084/7243093); [Hanson & Jones](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4453623/)). Men's friendships rely less on openness ([Oswald](https://www.proquest.com/docview/224662281)). *Moderate.* | Walk-and-talk, moving together, cooking, building and volunteering formats come before "let's catch up." |
| **12. Treat friends' data as borrowed** | People affected but not signed up are "indirect stakeholders" ([VSD](https://vsd.ccs.neu.edu/introduction/resources.files/Value_Sensitive_Design.pdf)). Location data has been resold ([SAN](https://san.com/cc/gen-z-endorses-location-sharing-associates-it-with-safety/)). Sharing circles signal who belongs ([CSCW 2026](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Like-Guardian-Angels-Continuous-Location-Sharing-and/991022175160604721)). Disclosing to bots tracks lower well-being ([Stanford](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138850)). *Principle plus cautionary cases.* | Match contacts on the device. Encrypt notes. Presence sharing is two-way and expires. No inferred "relationship health" scores. Never sell or monetize who knows whom. |
| **13. If AI appears, make it a bridge, never a companion** | Heavy companion use tracks with loneliness and dependence ([MIT/OpenAI](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2); [Psych. Science 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41870975/)). Manipulative goodbyes ([De Freitas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258v3)). California's SB 243 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/13/california-becomes-first-state-to-regulate-ai-companion-chatbots)). *Growing evidence, mostly correlational.* | AI drafts the text, suggests a time and offers conversation prompts. No persona, no open-ended emotional chat, and a neutral one-tap exit. |

### What this means for the shared feed

Your team's shared feed needs its own verdict. It is both the asset that solves the cold-start problem and the feature most likely to become a destination. Passive awareness of group members can create a sense of connection cheaply, but passive consumption is where the evidence turns negative:

- Passive scrolling predicted lower well-being when people had already been alone that day ([KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/02/12/study-dispels-notion-social-media-displaces-human-contact)).
- Passive browsing has been tied to upward comparison and envy ([Verduyn et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/492748767.pdf)). That is a live risk in a feed of other people's workouts. Critics argue the active-versus-passive split is too crude and that the quality of interactions matters more ([Valkenburg et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14614448211065425)).
- STEP UP's sponsors, who only saw weekly progress reports, produced no lasting change.

The prescription has four parts:

1. **Treat every post as a possible invitation**: "walking at 7, who's in?" or "call me on my run."
2. **Replace likes with responses that create plans**, such as "I'm in" and "call me."
3. **End the feed with a clear "you're caught up" state.**
4. **Anchor the feed's check-ins to one live weekly touchpoint per group**, held in person or by call.

The metric to run the feed by is the share of posts that end in a real-world handoff.

## Open questions worth testing

Almost none of the specific features above has been tested for social contact, which turns the gaps into a research agenda. Run these as A/B tests or micro-randomized trials, which randomize each prompt as it is sent. Use behavioral outcomes (calls placed, meetups attended) and validated scales such as the three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale, not before-and-after self-reports, which inflate effects. The WHO is proposing a global Social Connection Index ([WHO EB158](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/EB158/B158_28-en.pdf)), which suggests standard measures are coming. Aligning with them early will make your results legible to institutional buyers.

| Open question | Why it is unresolved | How to test it |
|---|---|---|
| Does letting feed posts work as invitations increase real meetups and calls? | No peer-reviewed study of handoff-first apps measures offline contact as an outcome. | A/B test invitation posts against the standard feed. Outcomes: occasional "did you meet or call?" check-ins, plus handoffs to the calendar and dialer. |
| Does copy that corrects pessimistic predictions increase calls placed? | The prediction errors hold up in the lab, but no in-app nudge has been tested. | Randomize the copy shown at the moment of reach-out. Measure completed calls and post-call "better than expected" ratings. |
| When should contact be scheduled, and when spontaneous? | Surprise raises appreciation; unpredictability raises anxiety. | Randomize scheduled versus spontaneous prompts by closeness of the relationship. Measure completion, the recipient's response and the sender's anxiety. |
| Are voice notes a bridge to live calls or a substitute for them? | No controlled study compares recorded voice messages with calls or text. Hearing a voice humanizes ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm)), but a voice note has no back-and-forth. | Track how often voice notes lead to calls. Compare closeness ratings across channels. |
| How often should relationship nudges come, and should the rate adapt? | The batching evidence is about notifications in general, and fixed daily prompts failed in a health app ([JMIR mHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)). | Randomize timing and frequency, with automatic slowdown when ignored. Watch mute and uninstall rates. |
| Do private reminders of a relationship's rhythm help, or do they breed guilt? | Evidence on streaks and tracking warns of costs, and no RCT compares guilt framing with warmth framing. | Three-arm test: no reminder, rhythm reminder, warmth-framed reminder. Measure contact, guilt and enjoyment. |
| Does three-person competition on health goals help or hurt connection for lower performers? | STEP UP's competition effect lasted, but the risk of discouraging people was not measured ([STEP UP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498375/)). | Opt-in competition arm. Track dropout in the bottom third and closeness ratings. |
| What group size, season length and host role best support retention and closeness? | No RCT compares recurring small groups with one-off events, and the effect of a host has not been measured. | Randomize group sizes of 3, 5 and 8, and a rotating host versus no host. Measure attendance and closeness. |
| Do effects last after the prompts stop? | STEP UP's support effects faded, and the effect of pairing workouts with a treat (here, audiobooks) wears off ([Milkman 2014](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)). | Withdraw prompts from some groups and follow up at three to six months. |
| Do chronically lonely users need a different path? | Effects are larger for people who start out lonelier, and hypervigilance to rejection can make invitations backfire ([Hawkley & Cacioppo](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874845)). | Screen with the three-item UCLA scale at onboarding. Test content that reframes social situations against simply sending more invitations. |
| Who pays: users, employers or health plans? | No willingness-to-pay data exists for friendship-maintenance tools, and institutions require outcome evidence. | Pricing tests. Employer or health-plan pilots with preregistered outcomes, combining a loneliness scale with behavioral outcomes. |
| Where is the "creepy" line for relationship notes and presence sharing? | No research on how people perceive personal CRMs was found. | Qualitative interviews, plus opt-in and pause rates for each data feature. |

## Where the strongest opportunities lie

### Turn accountability groups into standing rituals

This is the strongest opportunity. Your existing small groups already solve the three problems that kill friendship startups:

- **No cold start.** The network already exists.
- **A shared activity.** The common health goal gives people something to do side by side, which makes showing up easier, especially for men.
- **A ritual.** A weekly rhythm turns the group into a routine that builds up Hall's hours.

The move the evidence supports is to add a live layer to the asynchronous feed:

- A weekly walk-and-talk or move-together session for groups of three to six.
- A "make it recurring" default after any successful meetup.
- Plan prompts with a date, time and place.
- One reminder 30 minutes before.
- Present mode during the session.

Several signals support this. STEP UP suggests that light, opt-in competition in groups of three keeps health gains lasting. Pie's three-event retention suggests that early repeat attendance is the activation metric to watch. And the market's shift toward persistent groups means you can start where competitors are only now arriving.

Charge for the ritual itself, for example premium groups, hosted seasons, or venue and brand sponsorship along the lines of 222 and Hinge. Build that payment moment in from day one, because Meetup and Marco Polo drew backlash when they added fees later ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/users-upset-after-video-messaging-app-marco-polo-disseminates-premium-version_id124318)).

### Own "make the call happen"

Voice is the cheapest high-value channel, no product owns it, and its main barrier is a correctable prediction error. A voice layer would be both new and directly grounded in the evidence. It would let group members and close friends:

- post "free to talk" windows;
- schedule ten-minute catch-ups with a set length;
- pair calls with walks;
- use voice notes as a stepping stone, with a "call back now" button;
- see copy before the call that corrects their pessimistic expectations.

It extends naturally beyond the group to a user's inner circle of about 5 and next circle of about 15. Willingness to pay is unproven. This probably works best as the engagement core of a paid ritual product or an institutional offering, not as a standalone subscription.

### Sell measured connection to employers and health plans

A health company is better placed than a social startup to reach institutional payers. Health plans already buy connection services per member per month through companies like Papa and Wider Circle, and employers already budget for well-being. The prerequisite is outcome evidence: preregistered pilots that measure calls and meetups that actually happened, alongside validated loneliness scales. Fully remote workers (25% daily loneliness) and adults aged 45–59 (46% lonely) are underserved segments that employers and plans can reach. The risks are:

- long sales cycles;
- regulatory scrutiny of Medicare Advantage supplemental benefits;
- senior populations that often need support delivered by people, not apps.

### Make presence a feature, not the business

Phone-free socializing is a cultural trend with weak direct evidence and the economics of running events. A present mode, phone-free group sessions, and partnerships with the app-blocking ecosystem would set the product apart without turning the company into an events business. The pitch to blocker users is "what to do with the time you just got back," which Castelo's trial supports. Supporting people who use basic phones or Focus modes, through SMS, email digests and calendar invites, reaches a motivated and growing segment.

### What to avoid

Avoid four tempting paths:

1. **Swipe-style stranger matching.** Revenue is small, the list of failures is long, and safety with minors is a real exposure: Wizz, a friend-finder app for teens, was pulled from app stores over sextortion ([Infosecurity](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/wizz-removed-apple-google-stores)).
2. **An AI companion or persona.** There is growing correlational and regulatory evidence that companions displace human contact.
3. **A feed that becomes the destination**, with endless scrolling, likes and public counts.
4. **A friendship CRM that scores relationships.** These drift toward professional networking and feel like surveillance.

## Conclusion

The evidence recasts disconnection as a problem of scheduling and prediction, not of matching. Most people have others they could see or call. What is missing is the recurring time slot that builds up hours, and the willingness to make the first move, which is held back by predictions that reliably err on the pessimistic side. That changes what a good connection product looks like. It is a scheduler, a corrector of bad predictions and a doorway. It is measured by the meetups and calls that happened, and it stays silent during the moments it creates. This also explains the market. Products that sell introductions face dating-app churn without dating-app willingness to pay, while products that own a ritual or an existing circle keep growing.

For this founder, two conclusions stand out. First, the existing shared feed is both the starting asset and the main risk. A feed delivers the feeling of connection, and the evidence on passive use, on AI companions and on STEP UP's passive sponsors says the feeling is not enough. Second, rigorous evidence is the scarce resource. No app has shown in a randomized trial that it increases in-person contact or reduces loneliness. A team that measures offline outcomes from day one builds a better product and also the only credible pitch to the institutional payers who fund connection at scale.
