# Communication Modalities, Devices, and the Quality of Human Connection

Scope note: These notes compare face-to-face, voice, video, text and social media on connection quality, and cover what disrupts or supports presence. They were compiled in November 2026. Several full-text hosts (frontiersin.org, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/PMC, research.vu.nl, static1.squarespace.com) were blocked in this session, and the web-search budget ran out before the last few planned queries. As a result, many effect sizes come from abstracts, press releases or search-indexed summaries rather than full texts. Each place where this applies is flagged.

## 1. How do face-to-face, voice, video and text compare on connection, closeness and understanding?

### Takeaway
Across the experimental evidence, the biggest gap is between **voice-based** media (in person, phone, video) and **text**. Voice produces stronger bonds and more "humanizing" impressions than text. Video adds little to connection beyond what audio already gives. People choose text because they *overestimate* how awkward a call will be, a correctable forecasting error. In-person contact is still the top-ranked modality in the studies that include it.

### Cited Findings
**Kumar & Epley (2021), "It's surprisingly nice to hear you," *JEP: General* 150(3), 595–607**
- Across experiments, voice-based interactions (phone, video chat, voice chat) produced stronger social bonds than text-based ones (email, text chat) and were *not* more awkward, although participants expected them to be — [Nautilus summary of abstract](https://nautil.us/people-are-discovering-the-joy-of-actually-talking-on-a-phone-237971); [author PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5394dfa6e4b0d7fc44700a04/t/5f5a8bd2bec7a45b9db0d41c/1599769555766/Kumar%20Epley%20(in%20press)%20Its%20surprisingly%20nice%20to%20hear%20you%20JEPG.pdf) (PDF could not be fetched in this session)
- In one study, about 200 people predicted what reconnecting with an old friend by email or by phone would be like and were then randomly assigned to actually do one. Phone callers felt significantly more connected and no more awkward. Predictions had understated the difference between media — [UT News](https://news.utexas.edu/2020/09/11/phone-calls-create-stronger-bonds-than-text-based-communications/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200911141713.htm)
- People's preference for texting was driven by predicted awkwardness. Those who chose text expected a call to be more awkward, and they were wrong — [UT McCombs news](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/)
- Video added little to connection beyond audio. Kumar: "Phone calls can create just as strong a sense of social connection as audio-visual media" — [UChicago News interview with Epley](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/nicholas-epley-explains-why-phone-calls-can-connect-us-better-zoom); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/for-a-better-connection-talk-instead-of-typing/)
- Caveat: the full paper could not be retrieved, so the exact per-experiment Ns and effect sizes (d) are **not verified** here.

**Schroeder, Kardas & Epley (2017), "The Humanizing Voice," *Psychological Science* 28(12)**
- Evaluators who *heard* someone explain a view rated that person as more thoughtful, rational and humanlike than evaluators who *read* a transcript of the same words. The effect was strongest when evaluators disagreed with the speaker — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797617713798/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm)
- Audio-only and audio+video produced similar evaluations. The voice (paralinguistic cues), not the face, carried the effect — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm); [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/could-it-be-her-voice-why-scarlett-johanssons-voice-makes-samantha-seem-human/)

**Sherman, Michikyan & Greenfield (2013), *Cyberpsychology* 7(2)**
- 58 young women (pairs of close friends) each held four conversations: in person, video chat, audio chat and IM. Bonding, measured by self-report and by coded nonverbal affiliation cues, was highest in person, then video, then audio, then IM. Participants felt connected in all conditions. IM bonding was significantly lower than in-person bonding even though emoticons and typed laughter helped — [Cyberpsychology article](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/4285); [UCLA CDMC](https://www.cdmc.ucla.edu/the-effects-of-text-audio-video-and-in-person-communication-on-bonding-between-friends/)
- **Flag:** the publisher's PDF carries a correction stating that some *self-reported* bonding differences (in person vs video, in person vs audio) were *not* significant. The robust gap is between text and the voice/face modalities — [publisher PDF](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/download/4285/33249?inline=1)

**Other modality comparisons**
- Meta-analysis (Ruppel et al., 2017, *JCMC*): self-disclosure is higher face-to-face than in computer-mediated communication (CMC). The gap is moderated by how disclosure is measured, by study design and by CMC type. The authors report "mixed support" for the leading CMC theories (e.g., hyperpersonal, social information processing). Pooled effect size not retrieved — [MetaTOC abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/95943-reflecting-on-connecting-meta-analysis-of-differences-between-computer-mediated-and-face-to-face-self-disclosure); [Zendy DOI 10.1111/jcc4.12179](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1111/jcc4.12179)
- Hall et al. (2023, *Communication Research*): high-quality *face-to-face* communication was more closely associated with daily well-being than electronic or social-media contact — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0)
- Older adults: well-being was highest after face-to-face contact, then telephone, then text-based digital interaction. Text may matter more for people with small networks (conference abstract) — [PMC11691346](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11691346/)
- Text *can* build closeness when it carries responsiveness cues. A 2025 PLOS ONE study found that messages with emojis were perceived as more responsive, and perceived responsiveness predicted closeness — [PMC12221085](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12221085/). A 2025 study found brief online chats increased closeness whatever the topic; exchange quality mattered more than topic — [PMC11869026](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11869026/)
- Neuroscience: a 2024 study, "Generation WhatsApp: inter-brain synchrony during face-to-face and texting communication," directly compares neural coupling in face-to-face vs texting conversation (its results could not be verified in this session) — [PMC10834538](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10834538/)
- Phone calls as an *intervention* (RCT): Kahlon et al. (2021, *JAMA Psychiatry*) randomized 240 Meals on Wheels clients to 4 weeks of brief, empathy-focused phone calls from 16 lightly trained callers aged 17–23, or to no calls. Calls were daily for the first 5 days, then at least 2 per week at the participant's choice. Loneliness improved by 1.1 points on the UCLA scale (95% CI 0.5–1.7) and by 0.32 on De Jong Gierveld. Depression (PHQ) improved 1.5 points and anxiety (GAD) 1.8 points — [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20210223/empathyoriented-telephone-program-benefits-adults-at-risk-for-mental-health-problems); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/mental-health/layperson-delivered-intervention-eases-loneliness-for-at-risk-2650836899.html)

**Underestimating others: barriers to reaching out**
- Liu, Rim, Min & Min (2023, *JPSP* 124(4), 754–771): across 13 experiments (more than 5,900 participants), people underestimated how much others appreciate being reached out to (by text, call or email). Recipients focus on their pleasant *surprise*, which initiators overlook. The underestimation is larger for weak ties — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/07/220711095206.htm)
- Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2021/22, *JPSP*, "Overly shallow?"): across 12 experiments (more than 1,800 participants), both deep and shallow conversations with strangers were less awkward and more connecting than expected. Mispredictions were largest for deep conversations, and people underestimated their partners' interest in depth. Connection differences between deep and shallow conversations were not significant in every experiment — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk)

### Inferences
- The highest-leverage design move is to shift people from **text to voice**, not necessarily from voice to video. Voice captures most of the connection benefit (Kumar & Epley; Schroeder et al.), has lower friction than video, and avoids the costs of video fatigue (see Q4).
- Since the main barrier is a *forecasting error* (expected awkwardness, underestimated appreciation, underestimated interest in depth), "expectation correction" is a plausible design mechanic. Examples: in-app messaging like "people who called instead of texted felt closer and it wasn't more awkward," or post-call prompts asking users to compare how the call went with how they expected it to go. This is an inference; I found no study testing such an in-app nudge.
- Text has a legitimate role as a *coordination and reach-out* channel. Liu et al. show that even a quick message is appreciated more than expected. The app can use text to initiate and schedule, while steering the substantive conversation toward voice or in-person contact.
- The ranking in-person > video ≈ audio >> text is consistent with "media richness" / "social presence" theories. The audio ≈ video finding suggests that the voice channel, not bandwidth as such, is what matters.

### Gaps
- Full-text effect sizes (d) for Kumar & Epley (2021) and the Ruppel et al. (2017) pooled estimate were not retrieved.
- I found no meta-analysis that pools *connection/closeness* outcomes across in-person, voice, video and text. Ruppel et al. covers only self-disclosure.
- Primary sources for media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986) and social presence theory (Short, Williams & Christie, 1976) could not be retrieved before the search budget ran out. Treat them as background framing only.
- Results of the "Generation WhatsApp" inter-brain synchrony study were not verified.

## 2. Does the mere presence of a smartphone degrade conversation quality, closeness and cognition? Replication status

### Takeaway
**Balanced verdict:** The "mere presence" effect on *conversation quality* (Przybylski & Weinstein 2013; Misra et al. 2014) has **failed at least two direct replications** and should be treated as unreliable. The "brain drain" effect on cognition (Ward et al. 2017) has also failed direct replications. Meta-analyses find at most a small effect, limited to working memory. In contrast, **actually using** a phone during a social interaction reliably, though modestly, reduces enjoyment, attentiveness and smiling (Dwyer et al. 2018; Kushlev et al. 2019; Kushlev & Dunn 2019). The design implication is to target *use and interruption*, not just visibility.

### Cited Findings
**Conversation quality: original studies**
- Przybylski & Weinstein (2013, *J. Social & Personal Relationships*): a phone placed nearby during conversations between strangers lowered relationship quality and partner closeness. Trust and perceived empathy were lower in meaningful conversations — summarized in [Crowley et al. replication record, FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/10510974.2018.1467941/)
- Misra et al. (2014, *Environment and Behavior*, "The iPhone Effect"): 100 café dyads in Washington, DC, observed for 10 minutes. When a device was on the table or in hand, connectedness averaged 5.05 vs 5.36 without a device, and empathic concern 5.51 vs 5.85 (7-point scales). The empathy drop was larger for close pairs. The design was **observational**, and less engaging partners may have reached for phones — [Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/studies/society/internet/iphone-effect-social-interaction-quality-mobile-device-presence); [Virginia Tech news](https://news.vt.edu/articles/2014/08/080714-ncr-misrasmartphonestudy.html); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0013916514539755)

**Conversation quality: failed replications**
- Crowley, Allred, Follon & Volkmer (2018, *Communication Studies*): phone presence did **not** significantly affect relational quality or empathy, and conversation meaningfulness did not moderate this. The authors point to changing phone-use norms as one possible explanation — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/10510974.2018.1467941/); [Allred page](https://uwosh.academia.edu/RyanAllred)
- Linares & Sellier (2021, *PLOS ONE*): a close replication of the 2013 procedure (2 experiments, 356 participants in 136 groups) **failed to replicate** the relationship-quality effects and found no effect on creativity — [PMC8189469](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8189469/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/3b02f5f389c7438eb9ef36fe6553e388)
- The FORRT replication atlas lists the 2013 study as having **two failed replications** — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0265407512453827/)

**Cognition ("brain drain"): original and replications**
- Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017, *JACR*): having one's own phone nearby reduced available working-memory capacity and fluid intelligence, with the largest costs for the most phone-dependent participants. The FORRT atlas lists **2 failed replications** — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/)
- Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022): a direct replication of Ward's Experiment 2 found no effect of phone location on OSPAN or go/no-go — [summarized in Parry/meta-analysis search record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375898901_Does_the_Mere_Presence_of_a_Smartphone_Impact_Cognitive_Performance_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Brain_Drain_Effect)
- Hartmann, Martarelli, Reber & Rothen (2020, *Consciousness and Cognition*): **no evidence** of brain drain on short-term or prospective memory — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect)
- Johannes, Veling, Verwijmeren & Buijzen (2019, *J. Media Psychology* 31(4), 214–225; often cited as 2018 preprint): a preregistered experiment with a Bayesian sequential design manipulated phone visibility and notifications during a stop-signal task — [Hogrefe DOI](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/1864-1105/a000248); [WUR repository](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/hard-to-resist-the-effect-of-smartphone-visibility-and-notificati). **Flag:** the brief and the wider literature cite this as a null (failed) result for visibility. The search-indexed summary I retrieved paraphrased the hypothesis rather than the result, so the direction should be confirmed at the DOI before quoting.
- Meta-analysis, Parry (2024): systematic review plus 6 meta-analyses (k = 56, n = 7,093). Mere presence negatively affected **working memory capacity only**, with null summary effects for the other cognitive functions. There was substantial heterogeneity and generally poor power. Conclusion: "limited support for a negative albeit smaller than theorized brain drain effect" — [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/does-the-mere-presence-of-a-smartphone-impact-cognitive-performan/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375898901_Does_the_Mere_Presence_of_a_Smartphone_Impact_Cognitive_Performance_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Brain_Drain_Effect)
- Meta-analysis, Böttger, Poschik & Zierer (2023): 22 studies found a small negative effect driven mainly by memory tasks, with no significant effect on attention. It pooled *presence and use*, so it cannot isolate mere presence — [PMC10525686](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10525686/); [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/107880/107880.pdf)
- A presence-only meta-analysis (33 studies, 4,368 participants) reportedly found no overall effect. Authorship is unconfirmed (possibly Hartanto et al., 2024) — [search-indexed summary via useclocked.app](https://useclocked.app/research/brain-drain-meta-analysis-2023/) (low-quality source; verify)

**Active phone use during interactions (more robust)**
- Dwyer, Kushlev & Dunn (2018, *JESP*): a field experiment with 304 people dining in groups of 3–5 friends or family, randomly assigned to keep phones out or put them away. With phones out, participants felt more distracted and enjoyed the meal about **0.5 points less on a 7-point scale**. Distraction mediated the effect. The authors called the effect small but real. An experience-sampling study (about 100 people, 1 week) found less enjoyment of face-to-face socializing when people were using their phones at the same time — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180226122506.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2018-02/sfpa-pos022718.php)
- Kushlev, Hunter, Proulx, Pressman & Dunn (2019, *Computers in Human Behavior* 91, 12–16): in a preregistered study, strangers waiting with their phones smiled less, including fewer genuine (Duchenne) smiles. One report gives 103 vs 149 seconds of smiling. All participants were college students — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/strangers-smile-less-to-one-another-when-they-have-their-smartphones-study-finds/); [BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kushlev-hunter-proulx-pressman-dunn-smartphonesreducesmilesbetweenstrangers-2019)
- Kushlev & Leitao (2020) propose three routes by which phones affect well-being: **displacement** (crowding out other activities), **interference** (disrupting concurrent activities) and **complementarity** (enabling otherwise unavailable access/info) — [arXiv 2005.09100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09100)
- Norms: in Pew's 2015 survey of 3,217 US adults, 89% of cell owners used their phone at their most recent social gathering, and 82% of all adults said phone use at gatherings at least occasionally hurts the conversation (37% frequently). 33% said it at least occasionally helps — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2015/08/26/chapter-4-phone-use-in-social-gatherings/)

### Inferences
- The app should not claim that "a phone on the table ruins conversation." That specific claim is contested and has failed replication. Defensible claims are that *using* phones during time together modestly reduces enjoyment and attentiveness through distraction, and that phubbing is perceived negatively (Q3).
- Interruptions (notifications, checking) are the actionable target. Examples are a "together mode" that silences notifications during scheduled meetups or calls, and the app not pinging users while they are with the person the app connected them to.
- Effects are small at the level of a single interaction (around 0.5 on a 7-point scale), but they recur daily, and people themselves report disliking them (Pew 82%). This supports framing phone-free time as a social norm people already endorse, not as a health claim.

### Gaps
- Original Przybylski & Weinstein (2013) Ns and effect sizes, and Linares & Sellier's equivalence tests or Bayes factors, were not retrieved (PMC blocked).
- Parry (2024) pooled effect size (g) for working memory not retrieved.
- I found no 2023–2026 preregistered replication of the *conversation-quality* mere-presence effect. I ran out of searches before checking.
- The user-supplied attribution "Johannes et al. 2018 failed replication" matches Johannes et al. (2019, *J. Media Psychology*), a cognitive/response-inhibition test, not a conversation-quality replication. The direction of its result needs a full-text check.

## 3. Phubbing and technoference in couples, families and parent–child interaction

### Takeaway
Partner phubbing is **consistently but modestly** associated with lower relationship satisfaction, intimacy and responsiveness, and with more conflict. That conclusion rests on a 2025 meta-analysis of 52 studies and a 2014–2024 systematic review of 55 studies (n ≈ 29,925). However, the evidence is mostly cross-sectional and self-reported. Experiments show phubbing lowers perceived communication quality by threatening belonging. In families, parental technoference and child behavior problems feed each other over time, and parents' phone use lowers their own felt connection with their children.

### Cited Findings
- Roberts & David (2016, *Computers in Human Behavior*): developed a 9-item Partner Phubbing Scale (n = 308) and tested it in a sample of 145 adults. Phubbing lowered relationship satisfaction through conflict over phone use, and affected depression indirectly via relationship and life satisfaction. 22.6% said phubbing caused conflict, and 36.6% reported feeling depressed at least some of the time. The design was a correlational survey — [Baylor Magazine](https://magazine.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2015/bad-connection); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/300435)
- Chotpitayasunondh & Douglas (2018, *J. Applied Social Psychology*): in an **experiment**, participants imagined a conversation via a 3-minute animation in which the partner phubbed them extensively, partially or not at all. More phubbing lowered perceived communication quality and relationship satisfaction, mediated by threatened belonging and by affect. N was 153 or 128, depending on the source — [University of Kent](https://www.kent.ac.uk/news/society/17584/phubbing-can-threaten-our-basic-human-needs-research-shows); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/psychologists-have-looked-why-phubbing-so-harmful-our-social-lives)
- McDaniel & Coyne (2016, *Psychology of Popular Media Culture*, "Technoference"): women who rated more technoference in their relationships reported more conflict over technology, lower relationship satisfaction and more depressive symptoms. In the model, conflict over technology mediated the link to relationship satisfaction — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269334974_Technoference_The_Interference_of_Technology_in_Couple_Relationships_and_Implications_for_Women's_Personal_and_Relational_Well-Being)
- Meta-analysis, Ni et al. (2025, *Frontiers in Psychology*): across 52 studies, partner phubbing negatively affected relationship satisfaction, marital satisfaction, romantic relationship quality, intimacy, responsiveness and emotional closeness. Pooled r values were not retrieved (Frontiers blocked) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1561159); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391457764_A_meta-analytic_study_of_partner_phubbing_and_its_antecedents_and_consequences)
- Systematic review of the first decade of research (2014–2024), *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (2026): 55 studies, 29,925 participants. There is a consistent negative association between partner phone use and relationship satisfaction, alongside a methodological critique (designs and measures vary widely) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826003301)
- McDaniel & Radesky (2018, *Child Development*): in 170 US families, parents' problematic digital technology use predicted technoference in parent–child interactions, which was linked with child behavior problems. Cross-sectional — [Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/cdev.12822); [PMC6185759](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6185759/)
- McDaniel & Radesky (2018, *Pediatric Research*, longitudinal): 183 couples with a child averaging 3.0 years old were surveyed at baseline and at 1, 3 and 6 months. Child externalizing behavior predicted later technoference via parenting stress, and technoference often predicted later externalizing behavior. The authors describe a **bidirectional, transactional cycle**: stressed parents withdraw into devices, and this worsens behavior — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180613102022.htm)
- Kushlev & Dunn (2019, *JSPR*): in a museum field experiment, parents told to maximize phone use felt less attentive and less socially connected to their children than parents told to minimize it. A 1-week diary study of 292 parents found phone use lowered attention quality, which predicted lower felt connection. The authors note that total abstinence isn't required — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/smartphones-can-prevent-parents-cultivating-feelings-connection-children); [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0265407518769387)
- **Unverified figure to avoid:** a widely repeated "r = −0.219 across 30 studies" attributed to a "Dwyer & McDonald (2018) meta-analysis" could not be confirmed to exist — [Couples Analytics (unverified secondary)](https://couplesanalytics.com/en/science/phubbing-is-quietly-wrecking-relationships-the-scientific-evidence)

### Inferences
- **Conflict over phone use** is the mediating pathway in the couple studies, so the friction comes from a *perceived norm violation*, not phone use as such. This suggests the app should support *negotiated, shared* phone norms (for example, both people opt into "together mode") rather than unilateral restriction.
- For parents, the evidence supports "attention quality" framing (less phone during kid time = feeling more connected), not abstinence. Kushlev & Dunn explicitly say abstinence is unnecessary.
- Because the evidence is largely correlational, causal claims in app copy should be limited to what the experiments show: phubbing lowers perceived conversation quality, and phone use lowers parents' felt connection.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes from Ni et al. (2025) were not retrieved.
- I found no RCT of a *couples or families* phone-norm intervention measuring relationship outcomes. None surfaced in the searches run.

## 4. Video calls: fatigue, when video equals or falls short of in person, and long-distance family use

### Takeaway
Video calls carry specific costs: self-view ("mirror anxiety"), close-up gaze, cognitive load of producing cues, and immobility. These costs fall harder on women. Video also measurably narrows creative ideation and dampens the social-brain response compared with in-person contact. For *connection*, video adds little over voice for adults. Video is most valuable where visual co-presence matters: **toddlers with distant grandparents**, and possibly older adults, among whom video-chat users had roughly half the later risk of depressive symptoms (observational).

### Cited Findings
- Bailenson (2021, *Technology, Mind, and Behavior* 2(1)) identifies four proposed causes of Zoom fatigue: (1) excessive close-up eye gaze, (2) cognitive load from producing and interpreting cues, (3) constant self-evaluation from self-view, and (4) reduced physical mobility. His fixes include hiding self-view, shrinking faces, and **making audio-only the default**. Explicitly **theoretical**: "almost none" of the arguments had been directly tested in videoconferencing — [Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/social-interaction/nonverbal-overload-theoretical-argument-causes-zoom-fatigue); [PDF](https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/bailenson-apa-nonverbal-overload.pdf)
- Fauville, Luo, Queiroz, Hancock & Bailenson (2021 preprint; 2023 *CHB Reports*) used the Zoom Exhaustion & Fatigue (ZEF) scale (15 items, 5 subfactors) with a convenience sample of about 10,000 (10,591 in the preprint, 9,787 in the published version). About 14% of women vs about 5.5% of men reported very or extremely high fatigue after video calls. **Mirror anxiety** (measured by self-report and by linguistic analysis) mediated the gender gap. The 2023 paper also implicates video-conferencing *usage dynamics* (per its title) and other nonverbal mechanisms, which women experienced more — [Stanford VHIL 2023](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/videoconferencing/video-conferencing-usage-dynamics-and-nonverbal-mechanisms); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/64e3fedfe38e481fbbb927ff75deb0cc); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/zoom-fatigue-women-higher-men)
- Ratan, Miller & Bailenson: in 613 adults, fatigue was 14.9% higher for women than men and 11.1% higher for Asian than White participants. Facial-appearance dissatisfaction helped explain mirror anxiety — [Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/videoconferencing/facial-appearance-dissatisfaction-explains-differences-zoom-fatigue)
- Brucks & Levav (2022, *Nature* 605, 108–112): 1,490 engineers at a telecom firm across 5 countries were randomized to in-person or video pairs. Video pairs generated fewer and less creative ideas because screen focus narrowed visual and cognitive scope, measured by eye-gaze and recall. Video pairs were **not worse at selecting** the best idea — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v605y2022i7908d10.1038_s41586-022-04643-y.html); [Nature News & Views](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-022-01044-z)
- Hirsch lab, Yale (*Imaging Neuroscience*, 2023/24, "Separable processes for live 'in-person' and live 'Zoom-like' faces"): compared with video faces, in-person faces produced more social-brain (dorsal) activity, longer gaze dwell time, larger pupil-indexed arousal, more theta power and **more cross-brain coherence**. Camera geometry breaks true eye contact. Sample was modest — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/zoom-conversations-social-neuroscience-24996/); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/science_news/2023/10/27/brain-zoom/2861698414461)
- Older adults: Teo et al. (2019, *Am J Geriatric Psychiatry*) analyzed 1,424 US Health and Retirement Study participants (mean age 64.8). Video-chat users had roughly **half the probability** of depressive symptoms 2 years later, whereas email, social network and IM users looked like non-users. The design was **observational**, and the lead author says face-to-face is "probably best of all" — [PDXScholar](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/sph_facpub/190); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/490362)
- Toddlers and distant family: Myers et al. (2017, *Developmental Science*, "Baby FaceTime") had 1–2-year-olds spend a week video-chatting live with an adult or watching prerecorded video of the same content. Toddlers learned from and responded to the *live, responsive* partner, imitating actions and learning words, not the recording — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/virtual-family-time-how-families-connect-via-video-chat/); [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/parenting-translator/202202/does-facetime-count-screen-time-young-children)
- Caveat: Troseth et al. (2018) found on-screen responsiveness *alone* did not support word learning. A co-present adult helping the toddler interpret the screen mattered — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02195/pdf)
- Families commonly exempt video chat with grandparents from screen-time limits, and the AAP provides guidance for video-chatting with infants and toddlers — [McClure/Barr et al. via USP](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/mod/resource/view.php?id=2750471); [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/tips-for-video-chatting-with-infants-and-toddlers/)
- For adult connection, video ≈ audio (Kumar & Epley 2021; Schroeder et al. 2017) — see Q1 sources: [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/nicholas-epley-explains-why-phone-calls-can-connect-us-better-zoom)

### Inferences
- **Default to voice** for adult one-to-one catch-ups, consistent with Bailenson's "audio-only default" recommendation and the finding that video ≈ audio for connection. Offer video as opt-in.
- If video is offered, **hide self-view by default** (the strongest-supported fatigue mechanism), keep calls short, and avoid stacking calls back to back.
- Reserve a strong "video" nudge for intergenerational and long-distance family use cases, especially grandparents and young children. Coach the co-present parent to help the child engage, since responsiveness alone isn't sufficient (Troseth et al.).
- For creative or planning tasks (e.g., planning a trip together), nudge toward in-person. Brucks & Levav show the in-person advantage is in *generating* ideas.

### Gaps
- No randomized trial was found comparing video vs voice vs in person on *loneliness or closeness* among older adults or long-distance families. Teo et al. is observational.
- Hirsch lab N and effect sizes were not retrieved.

## 5. Asynchronous voice/video (voice notes, Marco Polo-style) vs synchronous

### Takeaway
I found **no controlled study** that directly tests whether asynchronous voice notes or video messages build connection comparably to live calls or to text. Indirect evidence suggests voice notes may carry some of the "humanizing voice" benefit, because that benefit comes from paralinguistic cues rather than from interactivity. They lack the turn-by-turn responsiveness that drives bonding, though. Young adults increasingly favor voice notes.

### Cited Findings
- Schroeder et al. (2017) humanizing-voice effects come from hearing a voice (audio ≈ audio+video). These were one-way, *recorded* audio stimuli, which is structurally similar to a voice note — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797617713798/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/11/171106112228.htm)
- Uswitch 2024 (2,000 UK adults): 37% of 18–34-year-olds favor voice notes, vs about 1% of 35–54-year-olds. Industry survey — [Jang/Uswitch summary](https://jang.com.pk/en/54151-gen-z-millennials-phone-phobia-70-young-adults-prefer-texts-over-calls-news)
- In online medical consultations (observational platform data, China), physicians' voice replies were associated with higher patient satisfaction than text, with the strongest effect for empathy and affirmation — [HEC Paris working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ebg/heccah/1592.html)
- Live responsiveness matters for learning in toddlers: live video chat outperformed pre-recorded video (Myers et al. 2017), so asynchronous video may lose part of the benefit for children — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/virtual-family-time-how-families-connect-via-video-chat/)
- **Unverified claim to avoid:** a voice-app company blog claims a "2021 Psychological Science" study found voice messages conveyed more warmth than text. It could not be verified — [Mindfuse blog](https://mindfuse-website.pages.dev/blog/voice-vs-text-connection)

### Inferences
- Treat voice notes as a **bridge modality**. They are lower in anxiety than live calls (asynchronous, controllable; see Q8), more humanizing than text, and potentially a stepping stone toward live calls for phone-anxious users. This is inference; it has not been tested.
- Since responsiveness drives bonding and learning, voice notes should be designed to invite a reply and escalate to a live call (e.g., "reply live" or "call back now" affordances).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment comparing voice notes or async video (Marco Polo-style) vs text vs live calls on closeness was found. This is a clear evidence gap and a potential opportunity for the app's own A/B research.

## 6. The displacement hypothesis, and active vs passive social media use

### Takeaway
At the **individual level**, short- and medium-term longitudinal data show that social media use does *not* reliably displace face-to-face or phone contact (Hall et al.). At the **population level**, in-person socializing has fallen sharply: teens spent about 1 hour/day less in person in 2016 than in the late 1980s, and US adults' social engagement declined between 2003 and 2020. Associations between tech use and well-being are tiny on average (≤0.4% variance). The active-vs-passive distinction is directionally useful but has been critiqued as too crude; interaction *quality and context* matter more.

### Cited Findings
- Hall, Kearney & Xing ("Two tests of social displacement through social media use," *Information, Communication & Society*, online 2018; often cited as 2019). In Study 1 (LSAY panel, 2009 vs 2011, adults born in the 1970s–80s), changes in social media use did not predict changes in direct contact (visits, calls, group meetings). In Study 2 (experience sampling, 116 participants, 5 prompts/day for 5 days), earlier social media use did not predict later face-to-face or phone contact. **Passive** use predicted lower well-being when the person had been alone earlier in the day. Critique: the samples were Gen X and college students, not adolescents — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/02/12/study-dispels-notion-social-media-displaces-human-contact); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/460287)
- Twenge, Spitzberg & Campbell (2019, *JSPR* 36(6), 1892–1913) analyzed about 8.2 million US adolescents (1976–2017). College-bound seniors in 2016 spent about **1 hour/day less** in in-person social interaction than in the late 1980s, and loneliness rose sharply after 2011. Teens low in in-person interaction and high in social media use were loneliest. Displacement appears at the **cohort level**; at the **individual level**, in-person interaction and digital media use were *positively* correlated. The data are correlational — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407519836170); [National Affairs digest](https://www.nationalaffairs.com/blog/detail/findings-a-daily-roundup/down); [PhillyVoice/The Conversation](https://www.phillyvoice.com/teens-depression-loneliness-friends-social-media/)
- Orben & Przybylski (2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*) ran specification-curve analysis on 3 large datasets (YRBS, Monitoring the Future, Millennium Cohort Study). Digital tech use explained **at most 0.4%** of the variance in adolescent well-being, a negative association comparable to eating potatoes. Sleep and breakfast showed stronger associations — [Oxford news](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/1434409); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2019-01/uoo-tue011419.php)
- Kannan & Veazie (2023, *SSM–Population Health*), using the American Time Use Survey (2003–2020), found social isolation increased and engagement with family, friends and "others" decreased, with men declining more steeply. Work hours constrained engagement — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36618547/); [US Surgeon General graphic](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-social-connection-graphic-trends.pdf)
- A 2026 analysis (Neal Caren) of time-use data reports that expected time with friends fell by more than half over the period studied. This is a blog-level summary that has not been verified — [Marginal Revolution, Aug 2026](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/the-end-of-friday-nights-with-friends.html)
- Verduyn et al. (2017) review: **passive** social-networking-site use (browsing) undermines well-being via upward social comparison and envy. The passive–harm link was more robust than the active–benefit link — [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/492748767.pdf)
- Verduyn, Gugushvili & Kross (2022, extended active–passive model): active use is not always beneficial and passive use is not always harmful. The effects depend on subtypes and user characteristics — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100143811372_0x000001); [KU Leuven Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/handle/123456789/560367)
- Valkenburg, van Driel & Beyens (2022, *New Media & Society*) is a critical scoping review of 2017–2021 studies questioning the evidence for the active/passive dichotomy. Valkenburg, Beyens & Pouwels (2024) argue that the *quality and context* of social media interactions matter more than active vs passive — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14614448211065425); [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/68871720/14614448211065425.pdf)
- Meier & Krause (2022, *J. Media Psychology*): an adversarial dialogue on whether passive use harms well-being — [FAU repository](https://open.fau.de/handle/openfau/23191)

### Inferences
- For product positioning: the app does not need to argue that "screens steal your friends." The individual-level evidence doesn't support strong displacement. The stronger argument is that **in-person time has declined at a societal level** and that **quality face-to-face contact** has the strongest link to well-being (Hall 2023). The app's job is to *add* such contact, not just subtract screen time.
- Hall's finding that passive use is especially bad *when one is already alone* suggests an intervention moment: detect or ask about "alone + scrolling" moments and offer a low-friction call prompt. This is inference and untested.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes from Hall et al. and Kannan & Veazie (minutes per day with friends) were not retrieved from full text.
- A 2023 meta-analysis on active/passive use (Bayer et al.) was mentioned in search results but not retrieved.

## 7. What makes an in-person interaction feel present and high-quality, and do phone-free gatherings help?

### Takeaway
High-quality presence is built from **attention, responsiveness and perceived listening**. High-quality listening has medium-to-large effects on speakers' self-insight and openness. A single intentional quality conversation measurably improves daily well-being. Eye contact and in-person gaze engage social-brain systems more than video does. Phone-free events are growing explosively (Eventbrite: +567% listings, 2024→2025). However, **peer-reviewed evidence that phone-locking pouches or Offline Club-style events improve enjoyment is essentially absent**. The best causal evidence remains Dwyer et al.'s phones-away dinner experiment (small but significant gain).

### Cited Findings
**Ingredients of presence**
- Hall et al. (2023, *Communication Research*): more than 900 students at 5 campuses were randomized to have one of 7 kinds of quality conversation with a friend in a single day: catching up, meaningful talk, joking around, showing care, listening, valuing opinions, or sincere compliments. Any one conversation improved end-of-day well-being, with the strongest effects on connection and reduced stress, and more conversations were better. **The type didn't matter; intentionally reaching out did.** Face-to-face contact was more strongly associated with well-being than electronic contact — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/02/230202135217.htm)
- High-quality listening (Kluger & Itzchakov) is defined as attention, comprehension and positive intention. In a meta-analysis (N = 952), being listened to well increased speakers' self-insight (d = 1.19) and openness (d = 0.46) and reduced prejudiced attitudes (d = 0.32). A 2025 paper found a meta-analytic effect of 0.20 on willingness to interact again — [JESP via X-MOL](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1304358820729032704); [EconPapers 2025](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cog:meanco:v13:y:2025:a:9871)
- Some listening effects are mixed: a 2024 registered report found listening increased positivity resonance but not perceived authenticity or state anxiety — [Moin et al. 2024, Reading](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/120196/9/moin-et-al-2024-the-effects-of-listening-on-speaker-and-listener-while-talking-about-character-strengths-an-open.pdf)
- Dutton & Heaphy's "high-quality connections" (HQC) framework names three pathways: **respectful engagement** (conveying presence and genuine attention), **task enabling** and **trust**. Heaphy & Dutton (2008) link positive interactions to physiological benefits. This is largely a conceptual/practitioner literature — [U-Michigan News](https://news.umich.edu/?p=5317); [Aalto University](https://www.aalto.fi/en/oasis-of-radical-wellbeing/corrosive-interaction-or-high-quality-connections)
- Eye contact and gaze: in-person face gaze produced longer dwell time, more arousal and more cross-brain coherence than video faces (Hirsch lab) — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/zoom-conversations-social-neuroscience-24996/)
- Attention is the mechanism behind phone harms: in Dwyer et al. (2018) distraction mediated the enjoyment drop, and in Kushlev & Dunn (2019) attention quality mediated parents' felt connection — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180226122506.htm); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/smartphones-can-prevent-parents-cultivating-feelings-connection-children)
- Going deeper than small talk is more rewarding than people predict (Kardas, Kumar & Epley) — [APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers)

**Phone-free gatherings**
- Device-free dinner RCT: Dwyer, Kushlev & Dunn (2018), with phones away vs out, showed about 0.5/7 higher enjoyment and less distraction (n = 304) — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2018-02/sfpa-pos022718.php)
- Eventbrite data (2025): phone-free event listings rose about **567%** globally from 2024 to 2025 and attendance about **121%**, spreading from 5 to 12 countries. The UK led, with listings up about 1,200% and attendance up about 1,441%. US figures conflict across outlets (listings +337% or +388%, attendance +913%). This is industry data — [Mixmag](https://mixmag.net/read/phone-free-club-events-up-by-567-new-data-reveals-news); [Fitt Insider](https://insider.fitt.co/phone-free-events-restore-connection-eventbrite/)
- Yondr (phone-locking pouches) reports more than 10,000 events and more than 20 million devices secured. These are **company-reported** figures — [Wikipedia: Yondr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yondr)
- The Offline Club was founded in Amsterdam by Ilya Kneppelhout, Valentijn Klok and Jordy van Bennekom. It began with offline weekends around 2022 and formalized café "hangouts" in February 2024. The format pairs about 1 hour of silent phone-free activity (reading, crafts) with about 1 hour of conversation, at roughly $17 per ticket. A London chapter followed in late 2024, with reported events in Paris, Barcelona, Milan, Berlin, Copenhagen, Dubai and elsewhere; city counts vary by source. The club reportedly gained 125,000 Instagram followers in a month — [The World (PRX), June 2024](https://theworld.org/stories/2024/06/13/this-club-in-the-netherlands-offers-a-much-needed-break-from-technology); [Dazed](https://www.dazeddigital.com/life-culture/article/66400/1/the-offline-club-event-series-phones-banned-smartphones-iphones-digital-detox)
- **Unverified:** a "2025 Northeastern University study" reportedly found higher emotional intensity and recall at phone-free concerts. The primary study was not found — [Fitt Insider (secondary)](https://insider.fitt.co/phone-free-events-restore-connection-eventbrite/)

### Inferences
- The app can operationalize HQC/listening research as **light-touch conversation scaffolds**: prompts drawn from Hall's 7 behaviors (catch up, joke, show care, listen, value opinions, compliment, meaningful talk) and deeper-question prompts (Kardas et al.), plus "listen fully" cues. Hall's result that *any* type works lowers the bar: the app should optimize for *intentional reach-out happening*, not for perfect conversations.
- Phone-free formats are a strong cultural tailwind and a plausible product feature (e.g., hosting or locating phone-free meetups). The app should present the evidence honestly: demand is booming, and phones-away has a small proven benefit to enjoyment, but pouch- or club-specific outcomes are untested.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of Yondr pouches or Offline Club events on enjoyment or connection was found. The Northeastern 2025 claim is unverified.
- No direct experimental test of Dutton & Heaphy's HQC pathways in friendship (non-work) settings was retrieved.

## 8. Phone-call decline, phone anxiety ("telephobia") and interventions

### Takeaway
Young adults strongly prefer texting: about 70% of UK 18–34s prefer text, about 25% say they never answer calls, and 56% assume an unexpected call means bad news. Peer-reviewed work (2025) links call anxiety to social anxiety, with texting preferred because it feels *controllable*. Interventions are mostly informal (role-play classes). The strongest evidence-based levers are **expectation correction** (calls are less awkward than predicted) and making calls **predictable and controllable**: scheduled, expected, low-stakes. These levers are inferred from the research rather than tested as telephobia interventions.

### Cited Findings
- Uswitch 2024 survey of 2,000 UK adults: about 70% of 18–34s prefer texts or social messaging to calls, 56% assume an unexpected call means bad news, and 37% favor voice notes (vs about 1% of 35–54s) — [Jang/Uswitch summary](https://jang.com.pk/en/54151-gen-z-millennials-phone-phobia-70-young-adults-prefer-texts-over-calls-news)
- Nearly a quarter of Gen Z/young adults never answer phone calls (2024 survey, as cited by Fortune). In a Sky Mobile 2023 survey, about a third of Gen Z rarely made calls and a quarter purposely ignored them — [Fortune, Feb 2025](https://www.fortune.com/2025/02/19/gen-z-is-afraid-of-talking-on-the-phone-college-launched-class-overcome-telephobia)
- Australian figures: a CommBank 2023 survey found 87% of Gen Z would rather text than call. Another survey of about 1,000 Gen Z Australians found almost 60% dread calls. Heavily repeated "90% anxious" figures are poorly sourced — [Murdoch University](https://www.murdoch.edu.au/news/articles/young-people-hate-making-phone-calls-could-it-be-hurting-their-careers); caution in [Subtext](https://subtext.it/blog/texting-anxiety-research) (note: commercial source)
- Peer-reviewed (2025), "Avoidance and Anxiety About Phone Calls in Young Adults: The Role of Social Anxiety and Texting Controllability": people in their 20s–30s had stronger texting preference and higher call anxiety than people in their 40s–50s. Social anxiety predicted perceived *controllability* of texting, which in turn predicted texting preference and call anxiety — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394609599_Avoidance_and_Anxiety_About_Phone_Calls_in_Young_Adults_The_Role_of_Social_Anxiety_and_Texting_Controllability)
- Related: "Predicting Telephone Anxiety: Use of Digital Communication Technologies, Language and Cultural Barriers, and Phone Avoidance" (2023) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371408438_Predicting_Telephone_Anxiety_Use_of_Digital_Communication_Technologies_Language_and_Cultural_Barriers_and_Phone_Avoidance)
- Intervention example: Nottingham College (UK) "Phone Anxiety Training" uses role-play and graded real calls (e.g., calling a restaurant to ask opening hours, asking a store about stock), and is also sold to employers. A careers advisor estimated that at least three-quarters of a class admit phone anxiety (anecdotal). There is no outcome evaluation — [Nottingham College](https://nottinghamcollege.ac.uk/employers/commercial-training/employee-training/phone-anxiety-training); [NBC/CNBC](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/business/money-report/gen-z-battling-with-phone-anxiety-are-taking-telephobia-courses-to-learn-the-lost-art-of-a-call/6152855/)
- Mechanism for intervention: people pick text because they *overpredict* awkwardness, and calls turn out better than expected (Kumar & Epley) — [UT McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/). Recipients appreciate outreach more than initiators expect (Liu et al. 2023) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/)
- Structured, scheduled calls work as a format: in the Kahlon et al. RCT, calls on a regular, scheduled cadence (daily for 5 days, then at least 2/week) by lightly trained young callers reduced loneliness, depression and anxiety — [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20210223/empathyoriented-telephone-program-benefits-adults-at-risk-for-mental-health-problems)

### Inferences
- Since *uncontrollability* and *unexpectedness* drive call anxiety (controllability study; "unexpected call = bad news" for 56%), the app should make calls **expected and bounded**:
  - a "call me" or "free to talk" availability signal
  - text-to-schedule ("call at 7?")
  - pre-agreed short durations ("10-minute catch-up")
  - optional topic prompts
  - an easy graceful exit

  This is a direct design translation; none of these specific features has been tested in published research that I found.
- Pair this with **expectation correction** (Kumar & Epley; Liu et al.) and a graded-exposure ladder: voice note → scheduled short call → spontaneous call → in-person. This borrows the role-play/graded logic of the Nottingham course and standard anxiety-exposure principles. It is inferred, not tested for this use.
- Users can be nudged to call **weak ties** without much fear of imposing, since underestimation of appreciation is largest for weak ties (Liu et al.).

### Gaps
- No controlled trial of a telephobia-specific intervention (scheduled calls, "call me" signals, low-stakes formats) was found.
- No Pew or Ofcom data on calling-vs-texting trends was successfully retrieved. Ofcom figures did not surface, and the most recent Pew data found was the 2015 etiquette survey. Survey statistics on Gen Z call avoidance are mostly industry or sponsored polls of uneven quality.
- The 2025 telephone-anxiety paper's sample size and effect sizes were not retrieved.
