# The Science of Human Social Connection and Loneliness: Scale, Stakes, and What Builds Relationships

*Research notes compiled 2026-11-05. Method note: in this session, direct page fetches (WebFetch) were blocked for most domains (who.int, americansurveycenter.org, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov all failed DNS resolution), so findings below come from web-search result extracts of primary sources (abstracts, official press releases, report summaries) plus well-established DOIs. Where a figure came only from secondary coverage, it is flagged. Every statistic is dated by survey/fieldwork period where known.*

**Definitions used throughout (important for product framing):**
- *Social isolation* = the objective state of having few relationships/roles/interactions; *loneliness* = the subjective, painful gap between desired and actual connection. The two are only moderately correlated and have partly independent health effects. — [WHO, Social isolation and loneliness](https://www.who.int/teams/social-determinants-of-health/demographic-change-and-healthy-ageing/social-isolation-and-loneliness); [Holt-Lunstad et al. 2015, Perspect Psychol Sci](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/7516)
- The 2023 US Surgeon General advisory frames social connection as three components: **structure** (number/variety of relationships, frequency of interaction), **function** (the degree to which others can be relied on for support), and **quality** (positive vs. negative aspects of relationships). — [US Surgeon General, *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation* (2023)](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) (not re-fetched in this session; framework widely reported)

## 1. What is the current scale of loneliness and social isolation (US and global), and how has it changed?

### Takeaway
Roughly 1 in 6 people worldwide (WHO, 2025) and about 1 in 5 US adults on any given day (Gallup, 2024) report loneliness, with broader "are you lonely" survey framings yielding 40–57% of US adults. The most robust trend evidence is behavioral: American Time Use Survey (ATUS) data show in-person time with friends has fallen by roughly half or more since 2003, steepest among 15–24-year-olds, and the decline continued through the 2025 data.

### Cited Findings

**Global (WHO and Gallup)**
- WHO Commission on Social Connection flagship report *"From loneliness to social connection: charting a path to healthier societies"* launched **30 June 2025**; estimates loneliness is associated with ~**871,000 deaths per year (~100 per hour)** and that ~**1 in 6 people worldwide** is affected by loneliness. — [WHO news release, 30 Jun 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death); [UN Geneva, Jun 2025](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/news/2025/06/107994/every-hour-100-people-die-loneliness-related-causes-un-health-agency); [Report summary (WHO)](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/from-loneliness-to-social-connection-summary)
- WHO's analysis drew on data from 153 countries; global loneliness prevalence = **15.8%** (data covering roughly 2014–2023); highest among **adolescents aged 13–17 (20.9%)**; objective social isolation estimated at **27% among adolescents aged 11–19**. Rates are reported higher in low-income countries. — [Science Media Centre Spain summary](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/one-six-people-worldwide-suffered-loneliness-last-decade); [Asia Economy coverage, Jan 2026](https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2026012208470193080); [News-Medical, 30 Jun 2025](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250630/One-in-six-people-worldwide-affected-by-loneliness-WHO-report-reveals.aspx)
- Policy timeline: WHO Director-General established the Commission in **Nov 2023** for a 3-year term (2024–2026). The **78th World Health Assembly (May 2025)** adopted resolution **WHA78.9, "Fostering social connection for global health"** — the first WHA resolution on social connection — asking Member States to integrate social connection into national health policy, data collection and awareness campaigns, with reporting to the WHA in 2027, 2029 and 2031. — [WHA78.9 text (apps.who.int)](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/WHA78/A78_R9-en.pdf); [Health Policy Watch](https://healthpolicy-watch.news/who-member-states-recognize-social-connection-as-a-global-health-priority/)
- **2025–2026 updates:** A WHO Executive Board report (EB158, for the Feb 2026 session) on the "Outcome of the WHO Commission on Social Connection" describes a proposed **global Social Connection Index** (a standardized international measure — still a proposal), a Technical Advisory Group meeting again in **mid-2026**, and exploration of a social-connection "policy lab." A social-connection side event was held at the **79th WHA (May 2026)**. — [WHO EB158/28](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/EB158/B158_28-en.pdf); [Foundation for Social Connection event listing](https://www.social-connection.org/event/social-connection-at-the-79th-world-health-assembly/)
- Meta–Gallup *Global State of Social Connections* (142 countries, fieldwork **Jun 2022–Feb 2023**): **24%** of people worldwide feel "very" or "fairly" lonely (>1 billion people); about half do not feel lonely at all; loneliest age group **19–29 (27%)** vs **17% for 65+**. — [Gallup, "Almost a Quarter of the World Feels Lonely"](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/512618/almost-quarter-world-feels-lonely.aspx); [Gallup analytics page](https://www.gallup.com/analytics/509675/state-of-social-connections.aspx)

**United States — prevalence**
- US Surgeon General advisory (**May 2023**): about **half of US adults** report experiencing loneliness; declared loneliness an "epidemic" (no new federal funding attached). — [STAT, 2 May 2023](https://www.statnews.com/2023/05/02/loneliness-surgeon-general/); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/loneliness-surgeon-general-epidemic-covid/); [Advisory PDF](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)
- Gallup (daily measure, "loneliness a lot of yesterday"): **~20% of US adults** in **Oct 2024**, up from 17–18% earlier in 2024; pandemic-era peak ~25%. — [ABC7 / Gallup, Oct 2024](https://abc7ny.com/1-5-us-adults-reports-daily-loneliness-gallup/15430562/); [KFF Health News Morning Briefing](https://kffhealthnews.org/morning-breakout/20-of-us-adults-say-they-feel-lonely-on-a-daily-basis/)
- Gallup World Poll (2023–2024 data, published **May 2025**): US national average daily loneliness **18%**. — [Gallup, "Younger Men in the U.S. Among the Loneliest in West"](https://news.gallup.com/poll/690788/younger-men-among-loneliest-west.aspx)
- Cigna Group *Loneliness in America 2025* (surveys fielded **29 May–14 Jun 2024**, Evernorth/YouGov/Morning Consult; UCLA Loneliness Scale-based): **57%** of US adults lonely; **Gen Z 67%**, millennials 65%, Gen X 60%, boomers 44%. Caveat: one-time snapshot; generational CIs not reported. — [Managed Healthcare Executive](https://www.managedhealthcareexecutive.com/view/more-than-half-of-americans-are-lonely-survey-shows); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/many-americans-are-lonely-and-gen-z-most-of-all-study-finds); [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/t-gen-z-loneliness-67-percent-baby-boomers-44-percent/)
- Harvard Making Caring Common (YouGov, fielded **May 2024**): **21%** of US adults report serious loneliness; ages 30–44 highest (29%), 18–29 at 24% (from HGSE summary; verify against report). — [Harvard GSE Usable Knowledge, Oct 2024](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/24/10/what-causing-our-epidemic-loneliness-and-how-can-we-fix-it)
- AARP (Ipsos, n=3,276 adults 45+, fielded **Aug 4–19, 2025**, released **Dec 3, 2025**): **40%** of adults 45+ are lonely, up from **35% in 2010 and 2018**. — [AARP press release](https://press.aarp.org/2025-12-03-New-AARP-Research-Loneliness-is-on-the-Rise); [AARP Research](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/relationships/loneliness-social-connections-2025/)
- Note on measurement: prevalence figures range from ~18–21% (daily-affect or "serious loneliness" items) to 40–57% (UCLA Loneliness Scale cutoffs). These are not comparable across surveys and should not be presented as a trend line. — derived from the sources above.

**United States — friendship structure ("friendship recession")**
- Survey Center on American Life, *American Perspectives Survey* (**May 14–23, 2021**, n=2,019): **12%** of Americans report **no close friends**, vs **3% in 1990**; 59% have a best friend vs 75% in 1990; share with **10+ close friends** fell from **33% (1990) to 13% (2021)**. Men with no close friends 3% → **15%**; women 1% → **10%**; men with 6+ close friends 55% → 27%. Caveat: sources differ on whether the 1990 baseline is a Gallup poll; question wording comparability not verified. — [Survey Center, *The State of American Friendship* (primary; not fetchable this session)](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/); [Denison Forum summary](https://www.denisonforum.org/biblical-living/if-youre-suffering-from-the-friendship-recession-youre-not-alone/); [Christian Standard](https://christianstandard.com/2021/08/a-friendship-recession); [Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/the-friendship-recession/601201786); [Focus on the Family Daily Citizen](https://dailycitizen.focusonthefamily.com/survey-finds-americans-increasingly-report-having-no-close-friends-especially-younger-men/)
- Survey Center *Generation Z and the Transformation of American Adolescence* (**Nov 2023**): only **40% of Gen Z** say they spent "a lot of time" with friends as teens; 78% of Gen Z regularly hung out with friends as teens vs 84% millennials, 89% Gen X, 88% boomers. — [Deseret News, 15 Nov 2023](https://www.deseret.com/2023/11/15/23958265/report-gen-z-teenagers-lonelier-more-apt-therapy-less-likely-use-drugs)

**United States — time-use trends (ATUS; strongest behavioral evidence)**
- Surgeon General advisory (ATUS 2003–2020): in-person time with friends fell from ~**60 min/day (2003) to ~20 min/day (2020)**; among **15–24-year-olds** it fell ~**70%** (commonly reported as ~150 min/day → ~40 min/day). Time spent alone rose ~24 hours/month while time with friends fell ~20 hours/month. — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/loneliness-surgeon-general-epidemic-covid/); [AJC](https://www.ajc.com/pulse/surgeon-general-loneliness-is-an-epidemic-with-profound-health-risks/XYHV5Z6SABDRFCEG4VOBAGYOMM/); [Advisory PDF](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)
- Kannan & Veazie (2023, *SSM–Population Health*), ATUS 2003–2020: social isolation rose and social engagement with friends and companionship declined, most sharply for young Americans; decline began before the pandemic. — [PubMed 36618547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36618547/)
- Time with friends per week was roughly flat at just under 7 hours/week (2003–2013), began falling ~2014, and was under 3 hours/week by 2021. — [The Ringer, "Why America Is Suffering a Friendship Recession," Nov 2022](https://www.theringer.com/2022/11/29/national-affairs/why-america-is-suffering-a-friendship-recession)
- Neal Caren (**2026**, *Socius*), ATUS 2003–2024 (N=243,095): friend time per adult fell from ~**24 min/day (2003) to ~9 min/day (2024)**, ~60% decline; the Friday-night peak has largely flattened so Friday now resembles a weeknight; losses concentrated from the mid-2010s onward. — [Caren 2026, doi:10.1177/23780231261478215](https://doi.org/10.1177/23780231261478215); [Marginal Revolution, Aug 2026](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/the-end-of-friday-nights-with-friends.html)
- **ATUS 2025 release (June/July 2026):** average daily time socializing (ages 15+) fell from ~45–47 min (2003) to **35 min (2025)**; **15–24-year-olds** fell from ~60 min/day to ~35 min; share of adults who socialized/communicated on an average day fell from **38% (2015) to 30% (2025)**; leisure games/computer/social-media time rose from 25 min (2015) to 37 min (2025). Caveat: Oct–Nov 2025 data missing due to federal shutdown. — [Axios, 5 Jul 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/05/americans-socializing-decline); [Deseret News, 31 Jul 2026](https://www.deseret.com/family/2026/07/31/bls-american-time-use-survey-2025-how-americans-lived/)
- Time spent attending/hosting parties and social events among 15–24-year-olds fell ~69–70% between 2003 and 2024 (ATUS). — [Reason, Jul 2025](https://reason.com/2025/07/11/americans-dont-party-enough-heres-how-to-change-that/); [Derek Thompson](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/the-death-of-partying-in-the-usaand)
- Counterpoint: some analysts argue the decline may predate ATUS (2003) and that reported *number* of friends has been steadier than *time* with friends — i.e., the change is more a loss of time together than a loss of friendships. — [Omnibudsman Substack](https://omnibudsman.substack.com/p/do-americans-even-make-friends-in)

### Inferences
- The clearest, least-contested signal for product design is **time together in person has collapsed**, especially unstructured weekend/evening time among young people. Self-reported loneliness prevalence is noisier and definition-dependent. An app should target *time spent together* (and its quality), not "loneliness scores" alone.
- The 2014–2015 inflection in ATUS friend time (pre-pandemic) coincides with smartphone/social-media saturation, but the cited sources do not establish causation; also implicated are closures of "third places" (Axios cites a 2025 CU Boulder report).
- Global policy momentum (WHA78.9, proposed Social Connection Index, 2027 reporting) suggests standardized connection metrics may emerge that an app could align with.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the frequently reported WHO figures of ~24% loneliness in low-income countries vs ~11% in high-income countries, or WHO claims about lonely students' grades / employment outcomes — the full WHO report and release pages could not be fetched.
- No Gallup national daily-loneliness figure for 2025 or 2026 was found; the latest confirmed is ~20% (Oct 2024) and 18% (2023–24 World Poll).
- Could not confirm a 2024/2025 American Perspectives Survey update to the 12% "no close friends" figure (one secondary source claimed 17% in 2024 — unverified, excluded). Search results referenced an Aug 2024 Survey Center report, *Disconnected: Places and Spaces*, on declining "third place" connection, but its figures and URL could not be retrieved.
- Exact BLS ATUS table values for 15–24 "time with friends" in 2024/2025 could not be pulled directly.

## 2. What are the health stakes, and which claims are robust vs. overstated?

### Takeaway
The association between weak social connection and earlier death is among the most replicated findings in social epidemiology (pooled odds/hazard ratios ≈1.14–1.50 depending on measure), and it is comparable in size to established risk factors — but it is observational, the effect of *objective isolation* is generally more robust than *subjective loneliness* after adjusting for health and depression, and the "15 cigarettes a day" line is a loose cross-study comparison ("up to 15"), not an equivalence.

### Cited Findings

**Mortality meta-analyses**
- Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010, *PLoS Medicine*): 148 studies, **308,849 participants**, mean follow-up 7.5 years; people with stronger social relationships had **OR = 1.50 (95% CI 1.42–1.59)** for survival, i.e., ~50% greater odds of survival. Effect consistent across age, sex, initial health status, cause of death, follow-up period. Complex measures of social integration showed the strongest association (**OR 1.91**); living alone vs. with others the weakest (**OR 1.19**). — [RTI summary](https://www.rti.org/publication/social-relationships-mortality-risk-meta-analytic-review); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/875360); [DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316)
- Holt-Lunstad et al. (2015, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*): 70 studies, ~**3.4 million** participants; adjusted pooled ORs: **social isolation 1.29, loneliness 1.26, living alone 1.32**; no significant difference between objective and subjective isolation, though the authors caution this "does not indicate interchangeability." — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/7516); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/social-isolation-epidemiology-7252/amp/)
- Wang et al. (2023, *Nature Human Behaviour*): 90 prospective cohort studies, **2,205,199** individuals; all-cause mortality pooled effect **social isolation 1.32 (1.26–1.39)**, **loneliness 1.14 (1.08–1.20)**; isolation also linked to cancer mortality (1.24) and CVD mortality (1.34). — [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01617-6); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20230707/social-isolation-loneliness-increase-risk-for-mortality)
- Steptoe et al. (2013, *PNAS*), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (n≈6,500, mean follow-up 7.25 y): after adjusting for demographics and baseline health, the most isolated quintile had **HR 1.26 (1.08–1.48)**; **loneliness was not significant (HR 0.92, 0.78–1.09)** and did not mediate the isolation effect. Authors: "We would not say that loneliness is unimportant," but reducing isolation is likely more relevant to mortality. — [PMC3625264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625264); [Nature news, 2013](https://www.nature.com/doifinder/10.1038/nature.2013.12673)
- WHO (2025) attributes ~871,000 deaths/year to loneliness and links social disconnection to stroke, heart disease, diabetes, cognitive decline, depression and anxiety; it also describes social connection as protective. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death); [Health Policy Watch](https://healthpolicy-watch.news/loneliness-social-isolation-linked-to-871000-annual-deaths-who-finds/)

**The "15 cigarettes a day" claim — provenance and caveats**
- Origin: Figure 6 of Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) placed the social-relationships effect size alongside effect sizes for other mortality risk factors drawn from *other* meta-analyses; the nearest bar was "smoking < 15 cigarettes a day." It was a side-by-side comparison of odds ratios from different literatures, not a direct comparison within one dataset. — [Holt-Lunstad 2010, PLoS Med](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316); [Substack critique, "Loneliness and the Apocryphal Fifteen Cigarettes"](https://whoisjohngalt.substack.com/p/loneliness-and-the-apocryphal-fifteen)
- The 2023 Surgeon General advisory phrased it as lacking social connection carries a mortality risk similar to smoking "**up to** 15 cigarettes a day" (AP initially misreported "12"). Fact-checkers rate the popular slogan "mostly true" with the caveat that "up to" is an upper bound, not an equivalence, and that it applies to premature mortality only. — [CBS News (AP correction noted)](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/loneliness-surgeon-general-epidemic-covid/); [Lenz fact-check](https://lenz.io/c/loneliness-health-risk-equivalent-smoking-15-cigarettes-1175b5c6)
- Additional caveats: (a) the 2010 estimate was for a composite of "social relationships," not loneliness specifically; (b) later, larger meta-analysis put loneliness's pooled effect at 1.14 (Wang 2023), well below the 2010 composite (1.50); (c) Steptoe (2013) found loneliness non-significant after adjustment. — [Wang 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01617-6); [Steptoe 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625264); Holt-Lunstad's own framing in [TED talk "Is social disconnection comparable to smoking?"](https://www.ted.com/talks/julianne_holt_lunstad_is_social_disconnection_comparable_to_smoking)

**Harvard Study of Adult Development**
- Began 1938; original cohorts of **268 Harvard sophomores** and **456 boys from poor inner-city Boston neighborhoods** (~724 men; now includes spouses and offspring). Director Robert Waldinger: "The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80" — relationship satisfaction at 50 predicted later health better than cholesterol levels at 50. Quality of close relationships mattered more than number of friends or marital status. Observational, originally all-male, mostly white. — [Christian Science Monitor, 2016](https://csmonitor.com/USA/Society/2016/0323/The-secret-to-a-happy-life-It-s-not-what-you-think); [Waldinger TED talk transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ted/what-makes-a-good-life-lessons-from-the-longest-study-on-happiness-robert-waldin?t=0); summarized in Waldinger & Schulz, *The Good Life* (2023)
- Harvard Chan School overview of the loneliness-to-connection research base and pandemic lessons. — [Harvard T.H. Chan School](https://hsph.harvard.edu/health-happiness/news/from-loneliness-to-social-connection-lessons-from-research-and-a-global-pandemic)

**Digital media**
- Surgeon General advisory cites a study finding people using social media **2+ hours/day** were more than **twice as likely** to report social isolation as those using it <30 min/day (correlational). — [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2023/05/02/loneliness-surgeon-general/)

### Inferences
- Robust: low social integration / objective isolation predicts mortality across dozens of cohorts and millions of people. Less robust: precise "equivalences" (cigarettes, obesity) and loneliness-specific mortality effects, which shrink after adjusting for health/depression.
- For product messaging, the defensible claim is "social connection is a major, independent predictor of health and longevity, on par with well-known risk factors" — avoid "as bad as 15 cigarettes" without "up to" and context.
- The fact that *objective* contact (structure) predicts mortality more robustly than *subjective* loneliness supports an app that drives **actual interactions**, not just mood-tracking.
- The WHO 871,000 deaths figure is a model-based attributable-mortality estimate built on observational relative risks; causal attribution is uncertain.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Holt-Lunstad's own written clarification of the "15 cigarettes" comparison (one secondary site claims it exists on her academic site — unverified).
- Could not verify the Harvard Study's specific published papers/effect sizes (only Waldinger's public summaries); results remain observational with a non-representative original sample.
- Randomized evidence that *increasing* connection *causes* lower mortality is lacking; not found in this search.

## 3. How are relationships actually built?

### Takeaway
Friendships are built through accumulated time — roughly 40–60 hours to become a casual friend, 80–100 hours to become a friend, and 200+ hours to become a close friend (Hall), with leisure/hanging-out time counting far more than working side by side — combined with escalating, reciprocal self-disclosure met by perceived responsiveness (Reis & Shaver). Proximity and repeated, low-stakes, unplanned exposure provide the opportunities; brief interactions with weak ties and strangers produce measurable, under-appreciated mood benefits.

### Cited Findings

**Time investment (Jeffrey Hall)**
- Hall, "How many hours does it take to make a friend?" (*Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, online Mar 2018; print 2019, vol. 36(4)). Study 1: **355 adults who had relocated within the previous 6 months**; Study 2: **112 new University of Kansas freshmen** tracked over time. KU release: **~40–60 hours** to form a casual friendship, **~80–100 hours** to become a friend, **>200 hours** to become a good/close friend (popularly rounded to 50/90/200). — [KU News, 6 Mar 2018](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend); DOI 10.1177/0265407518761225
- **Kind of time matters:** hours spent working together (e.g., coworkers) contributed much less to closeness than time spent hanging out/socializing in leisure contexts. Hall's later experimental work identifies everyday talk — catching up, joking, meaningful conversation — as the content that builds belonging (see Question 5, Hall et al. 2023). — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend); [Psychology Today (Hall blog)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-waves/201803/how-do-you-make-or-maintain-friends-put-in-the-time); [KU News 2023](https://news.ku.edu/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0)
- Caveat: self-reported/retrospective hour estimates; two samples (relocators and freshmen); ranges rather than precise thresholds. — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)

**Social brain / Dunbar's layers**
- Dunbar's layered model: cumulative circles of ~**5, 15, 50, 150** people with declining emotional closeness and contact frequency outward; supported by mobile-phone-call data (Mac Carron, Kaski & Dunbar 2016), though middle layers show large variability. Dunbar (2018, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*): "friendship is the single most important factor influencing our health, well-being and happiness," and creating/maintaining friendships is "extremely costly" in time and cognition. — [Dunbar 2018, PubMed 29273112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29273112/); [RealKM summary of layer evidence](https://realkm.com/2016/08/17/new-research-supports-layer-structure-in-social-brain-hypothesis/)
- **Contested:** Lindenfors, Wartel & Lind (2021, *Biology Letters*) re-ran the primate neocortex–group-size analyses with Bayesian/GLS phylogenetic methods; the 95% CI for a human group-size limit was so wide (reported ~2 to 520) that "it is not possible to make an estimate for humans with any precision." Dunbar disputes the methods. The critique targets "150," not the existence of layered closeness. — [Lindenfors et al. 2021, PMC8103230](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8103230); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2021-06-dispute-dunbar-people-relationships.pdf); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/scientists-say-you-can-have-more-than-150-friends-if-y-1846820957)

**Intimacy process model (Reis & Shaver, 1988)**
- Intimacy develops through an interpersonal process: one person **self-discloses**, the partner **responds**, and the discloser **perceives the response as understanding, validating and caring**. — [Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett & Pietromonaco 1998, JPSP 74(5)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/); [West 2010 (interracial friendships test of the model)](https://tessawestlab.hosting.nyu.edu/documents/West_2010_From%20strangers%20to%20friends%20The%20interpersonal%20process%20model%20of%20intimacy%20in%20developing%20interracial%20friendships.pdf)
- Laurenceau et al. (1998), event-contingent diaries over 1–2 weeks: self-disclosure and partner disclosure predicted felt intimacy; **perceived partner responsiveness partially mediated** the effect; **disclosure of emotions predicted intimacy more than disclosure of facts**. Replicated in married couples over 42 days of diaries (Laurenceau et al. 2005). — [Laurenceau 1998 PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/1998/LaurenFBPl1998.pdf); [Laurenceau 2005 PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2005/Laurenceauetal2005.pdf)

**Aron's "fast friends" / 36 questions**
- Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997, *PSPB* 23(4):363–377): unacquainted pairs who took turns asking **36 questions in three sets of 12 that escalate in personal depth** (~45 min) reported significantly greater closeness than pairs asking 36 small-talk questions. — [Greater Good in Action (procedure)](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness); [PsychCentral](https://www.psychcentral.com/relationships/want-to-be-close-to-someone-ask-these-36-questions); DOI 10.1177/0146167297234003
- The procedure is widely used as a lab "closeness induction" (e.g., Sprecher et al. 2013), but popular framing as a way to "fall in love" (2015 media wave) goes beyond the evidence, which measured short-term felt closeness. — [Business Insider (2015 popularization)](https://www.businessinsider.in/these-are-the-questions-one-psychologist-says-can-make-you-fall-in-love-with-a-stranger/amp_articleshow/45875357.cms); [Review citing Sprecher (JSPR 2021)](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.1177/0265407521996055)

**Proximity, propinquity, mere exposure**
- Festinger, Schachter & Back (1950), MIT Westgate housing (married student veterans): friendships were strongly predicted by physical and "functional" distance (e.g., units near stairwells/mailboxes). Commonly reported figures: **41% of next-door neighbors** were close friends vs 22% two doors apart and 10% at opposite ends of a hall (secondary source; original table not verified). — [Wikipedia, Propinquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propinquity); [GCN](https://gcn.com/?p=21369)
- Moreland & Beach (1992, *JESP*): four similar-looking women attended a large lecture 0, 5, 10 or 15 times without interacting; students rated those seen more often as more attractive/likable despite not consciously recognizing them — repeated exposure alone increases liking. — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html)
- Mere-exposure is not linear: a 2017 meta-analysis (Montoya et al., 268 curves) found an **inverted-U** — liking rises then declines with over-exposure; effects are strongest early in familiarity. — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html)

**Weak ties and micro-interactions**
- Sandstrom & Dunn (2014, *PSPB* 40(7):910–922): students were happier on days they interacted with more classmates than usual, whether ties were weak or strong; in a community sample, more weak-tie interactions related to greater well-being and day-level belonging. Correlational. — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/15300/); [World Database of Happiness](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/social-interactions-and-well-being-the-surprising-power-of-weak-ties-12167/)
- Sandstrom & Dunn barista study: customers instructed to have a brief genuine exchange (smile, eye contact, brief conversation) with a barista left in a better mood and felt more belonging than those told to be efficient. — [Psychology Today summary](https://psychologytoday.com/au/blog/dont-get-me-wrong/202311/why-small-talk-with-anyone-tends-to-be-so-rewarding)
- Epley & Schroeder (2014, *J Exp Psychol: General* 143:1980–1999), "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude": **9 studies** on Chicago trains, buses, taxis and a waiting room; commuters instructed to talk to a stranger reported a **more positive commute** than those told to sit in solitude or commute as usual; participants predicted the opposite, partly because they assumed others wouldn't want to talk. — [Chicago Booth press release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/mom-was-wrong-you-should-talk-to-strangers); [ScienceDaily, Jul 2014](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140717141851.htm); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/were-happier-when-we-chat-strangers-our-instinct-ignore-them)
- Deep vs. shallow talk: see Kardas et al. (2022) under Question 4 — deeper conversations with strangers produced more connection and enjoyment than expected.

**Mode of contact**
- Kumar & Epley (2021, *J Exp Psychol: General*), "It's surprisingly nice to hear you": voice-based interactions (phone, video, voice chat) created **stronger social bonds** than text-based ones (email, text chat), **with no increase in awkwardness**; people nonetheless preferred texting because they expected calls to be awkward. **Video added little beyond audio.** — [UT Austin McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/); [Author PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5394dfa6e4b0d7fc44700a04/t/5f5a8bd2bec7a45b9db0d41c/1599769555766/Kumar%20Epley%20(in%20press)%20Its%20surprisingly%20nice%20to%20hear%20you%20JEPG.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/phone-calls-help-create-closer-bonds-texting)
- Hall et al. (2023, *Communication Research*): face-to-face contact was more strongly tied to daily well-being than electronic or social media contact. — [KU News, 1 Feb 2023](https://news.ku.edu/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0)

**Being present (phones during interaction)**
- Dwyer, Kushlev & Dunn (2018, *JESP*): field experiment with **304** people dining with friends/family; randomly assigning phones to be present (vs. put away) **reduced enjoyment** and increased distraction and boredom; effect modest (~0.5 point on a 7-point scale). — [ScienceDaily, Feb 2018](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180226122506.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2018-02/sfpa-pos022718.php); DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2017.10.007

### Inferences
- **Design for hours, not taps:** reaching "friend" status takes on the order of 100 hours of mostly leisure time; a product that helps people repeatedly *show up* in the same places with the same people (recurring, low-stakes gatherings) attacks the binding constraint.
- **Recurrence + proximity + structure:** propinquity and mere-exposure findings argue for features that create *repeated, unplanned* encounters with the same people (standing weekly meetups, neighborhood/"third place" anchoring) rather than one-off matching events. The inverted-U warns against forcing over-exposure.
- **Escalating, reciprocal disclosure with responsive listening** is the core mechanism; question prompts that escalate gradually (Aron-style) and nudges toward *responsiveness* (reflecting, validating) — not just talking — map directly onto the evidence. Emotional disclosure > factual disclosure.
- **Voice over text:** where in-person isn't possible, the evidence favors nudging calls/voice notes over text; video adds little over audio.
- **Micro-interactions count:** prompts for brief, warm exchanges with weak ties (barista, neighbor, classmate) are cheap and evidence-backed mood boosters.
- **Presence:** the app's own design should minimize phone use *during* meetups (e.g., "phones away" mode), since phone presence measurably lowers enjoyment.

### Gaps
- Could not verify original Westgate percentages or Moreland & Beach's exact rating values against primary texts.
- No large preregistered replication of Hall's hour thresholds was found.
- Dunbar's layer-specific contact frequencies (weekly/monthly/yearly) were not verified in full text this session.

## 4. What psychological barriers stop people from connecting?

### Takeaway
A consistent, replicated body of work (largely Epley, Boothby, Sandstrom and colleagues) shows people systematically underestimate how much others want to talk, how much they are liked, how much a reach-out is appreciated, and how well deep conversation will go — while overestimating awkwardness. In chronic loneliness, these miscalibrations harden into hypervigilance for social threat, and interventions that target maladaptive social cognition outperform those that just add social opportunities.

### Cited Findings

**Underestimating appreciation for reaching out**
- Liu, Rim, Min & Min (*JPSP*, online Jul 2022; print 2023; DOI 10.1037/pspi0000402), "The Surprise of Reaching Out: Appreciated More Than We Think": **13 experiments, >5,900 participants**; initiators consistently **underestimated how much recipients appreciated** an unexpected call, text, email or small gift "just to check in." Recipients focus more on the **surprise** element, which drives appreciation. Underestimation was **larger for weak ties** than strong ties; when the contact was expected/routine, appreciation was lower and the underestimation disappeared. — [EurekAlert / APA release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/958120); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/story/lost-touch-with-someone-reach-out--your-friend-will-likely-appreciate-it-more-than-youthink)

**Underestimating interest in deep conversation**
- Kardas, Kumar & Epley (*JPSP*, online Sep 2021; 2022 volume), "Overly Shallow? Miscalibrated Expectations Create a Barrier to Deeper Conversation": **12 experiments, >1,800 participants**, mostly stranger pairs. People **overestimated awkwardness** and underestimated enjoyment and connection of deep conversations; they **underestimated how interested strangers would be** in their disclosures. In one study people expected to prefer shallow talk but after experiencing both preferred deep. — [APA press release, Sep 2021](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2021/09/deep-conversations-strangers); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-psychology-research-finds-deep-conversations-with-strangers-tend-to-go-better-than-people-expect)

**The liking gap**
- Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018, *Psychological Science* 29(11):1742–1756): across **5 studies**, people **systematically underestimated how much conversation partners liked them** and enjoyed their company. Observed with lab strangers, first-year dorm-mates (gap **persisted for months**, closing only by end of the school year), and a community workshop. Driven by preoccupation with one's own performance; negative self-thoughts predicted the gap. Data publicly available (OSF). — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=148177); [ScienceDaily, Sep 2018](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/09/180910160629.htm)

**The beautiful mess effect**
- Bruk, Scholl & Bless (2018, *JPSP* 115(2):192–205): **7 studies**; people judge their **own** displays of vulnerability (confessing feelings, admitting mistakes, asking for help) **more negatively** than observers judge the same displays by others, who see them as more courageous/authentic. Explained via construal-level theory (own = concrete, others = abstract). Caveat: mostly hypothetical scenarios (one real-life singing study); self-compassion reduces the gap. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/beautiful-mess-effect); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/the-beautiful-mess-effect-other-people-view-our-vulnerability-more-positively-than-we-do/)

**Fear of awkwardness and talking to strangers**
- Epley & Schroeder (2014): commuters predicted talking to a stranger would be less pleasant than solitude and that others wouldn't want to talk; actual experience was the reverse. — [Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/mom-was-wrong-you-should-talk-to-strangers)
- "Hello, stranger? Pleasant conversations are preceded by concerns about starting one" (2022, J Exp Psych: General) — anxiety concentrates on *initiating* conversation. — [PubMed 34618536](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34618536/)
- Kumar & Epley (2021): anticipated awkwardness drove people to text instead of call even when they knew calling would feel more connecting; Kumar notes the discomfort is concentrated in the first ~30 seconds of a call. — [UT McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/)
- **Intervention evidence:** Sandstrom, Boothby & Cooney (2022, *JESP*, DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2022.104356): a **week-long app-based scavenger hunt** (29 prompts, e.g., "find someone drinking coffee" and talk to them; n≈286 vs. control) **reduced fears** of talking to strangers and made predictions more accurate; repeated experience lets people discover conversations go well. — [SPSP Character & Context blog](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/sandstrom-boothby-cooney-talking-to-strangers); [PsyPost, Dec 2022](https://www.psypost.org/2022/12/psychology-researchers-find-that-a-simple-talking-to-strangers-intervention-is-surprisingly-effective-64561); [Sussex figshare](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Talking_to_strangers_a_week-long_intervention_reduces_psychological_barriers_to_social_connection/23490227)

**Maladaptive social cognition in chronic loneliness**
- Cacioppo & Hawkley's model (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2009; Hawkley & Cacioppo 2010): perceived isolation produces **hypervigilance for social threat**, with attention, memory and confirmation biases toward negative social information, which elicits behavior that confirms expectations — a **self-reinforcing loop** — while also raising desire to reconnect. — [Hawkley & Cacioppo 2010, PMC3874845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874845); [Bangee thesis summary, UCLan](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/16629)
- Empirical support is mixed: lonely young adults show visual vigilance to socially threatening stimuli (Bangee et al. 2014), but a naturalistic social-stress study found no marked difference in stress reactivity between high- and low-loneliness groups (Nowland et al. 2018). — [Bangee et al., UCLan](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/10947); [Nowland et al. 2018 PDF](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/id/eprint/23042/1/23042%20Nowland%20R%20%282018%29%20Loneliness%2C%20HPA%20Stress%20Reactivity%20and%20Social%20Threat%20Sensitivity.pdf)
- Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo (2011, *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 15(3)) meta-analysis of loneliness interventions: four strategies — improving social skills, enhancing social support, **increasing opportunities for social contact**, and **addressing maladaptive social cognition**; among randomized studies, interventions targeting **maladaptive social cognition** (e.g., CBT-style reframing) were the most effective; skills and opportunity interventions produced small effects at best. Overall effects were modest. — [PMC3865701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3865701); [Masi et al. PDF](https://louisville.edu/envirome/healthyairwaterandsoil/ubn2/selected-literature/Masi%20et%20al.%202011%20A%20Meta-Analysis%20of%20Interventions%20to%20Reduce%20Loneliness.pdf); [UChicago Medicine](https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/neurosciences-articles/research-shows-loneliness-poses-health-risk)

### Inferences
- The barriers are largely **forecasting errors**, which means an app can help by (a) **telling people the base rates** ("people appreciate check-ins more than senders expect," "your partner probably liked you more than you think"), (b) **lowering the initiation cost** (pre-drafted openers, scheduled calls, prompts), and (c) **creating repeated low-stakes practice** that corrects expectations through experience (the scavenger-hunt intervention is a direct product precedent).
- Liu et al. suggest **surprise matters**: purely routine, automated check-ins may lose much of their value; nudges should feel personal/spontaneous rather than templated "happy birthday" automation. Weak ties are where the appreciation gap — and opportunity — is largest.
- For chronically lonely users, simply surfacing more events may not help (Masi 2011); features that gently challenge negative social interpretations (e.g., reflection prompts after an interaction, liking-gap education) are better supported.
- "Beautiful mess" findings support prompts that normalize vulnerability (asking for help, admitting struggle) as a route to closeness.

### Gaps
- Large independent preregistered replications of the liking gap, Kardas et al., and Liu et al. were not located in this session (the original papers each contain many internal studies, which is reassuring but not equivalent to independent replication).
- Long-term durability of the scavenger-hunt intervention's effects (beyond ~1 week) was not verified.
- Later loneliness-intervention meta-analyses (post-2011) were not retrieved to check whether the "maladaptive social cognition works best" conclusion has held.

## 5. What does research say about maintaining relationships over time?

### Takeaway
Friendships, unlike kin ties, decay quickly when contact and shared activities drop; closeness is maintained through regular interaction plus positivity, supportiveness, and openness. Even one intentional quality conversation with a friend per day measurably improves daily well-being, and Dunbar's layered model implies the closest few relationships need roughly weekly contact.

### Cited Findings
- Roberts & Dunbar (2011, *Evolution and Human Behavior* 32:186–197): 18-month longitudinal study of **25 students** transitioning from school to university; the **emotional intensity of friendships was more sensitive than kin relationships to decreases in contact frequency and in shared activities**; friendships carry higher maintenance costs than kin ties. Small, single-transition sample. — [Bib-BVB record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_887497992); [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/131226437.pdf); DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.08.005
- Roberts & Dunbar (2011), cross-sectional study of **251 women**: communication frequency is shaped by kinship, network size and emotional closeness (findings not retrieved in detail). — [Oxford record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/oxford-uuid:41f6a45d-9d7d-4bb7-8ac2-cc13dd04a103?lng=en)
- Dunbar (2018): maintaining friendships is "extremely costly" in time; closeness layers correspond to declining contact frequency outward (innermost ~5 with the most frequent contact). Commonly cited rule of thumb from Dunbar's work: ~weekly contact for the inner 5, ~monthly for the 15, ~yearly for the 150 — **not verified in full text this session**. — [PubMed 29273112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29273112/)
- Oswald, Clark & Kelly (2004, *J Social & Clinical Psychology* 23(3):413–441): validated four **friendship maintenance behaviors — positivity, supportiveness, openness, interaction**; best friends engage in more of them than close or casual friends; women's same-sex friendships reported more supportiveness, openness and interaction than men's; both partners' maintenance predicted satisfaction and commitment. — [ProQuest abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/224662281); [Harvard Social Connection blog, "Friendship 101"](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/socialconnection/2023/03/20/friendship-101)
- Hall, Holmstrom, Pennington, Perrault & Totzkay (*Communication Research*, online 2023; print 2025 vol. 52(3)), "Quality Conversation Can Increase Daily Well-Being": three experiments (before, during, after lockdowns) randomly assigned people to one of seven communication behaviors (e.g., catching up, joking, meaningful talk, showing care, listening, giving a compliment, valuing their opinion) with a friend; **as little as one such conversation in a day improved end-of-day well-being** (mini-meta-analytic **d = 0.255**); more conversations → better days; *which* behavior mattered less than intentionally reaching out; face-to-face > electronic. Grounded in Hall's **Communicate Bond Belong (CBB) theory**. — [KU News, 1 Feb 2023](https://news.ku.edu/2023/02/01/just-one-quality-conversation-friend-boosts-daily-well-being-0); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/978536)
- Liu et al. (2022/2023): reaching out to people one has lost touch with is appreciated more than expected, especially weak ties — relevant to *reviving* dormant ties. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/958120)
- Hall (2018): relocation strongly disrupts friendship networks; new relationships require substantial hour investments to rebuild (sample of 355 recent relocators). — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)

### Inferences
- An app can operationalize maintenance as **cadence by tier**: identify a user's inner ~5 and next ~15 and nudge at roughly weekly/monthly rhythms, plus periodic "surprise" reach-outs to dormant weak ties (where appreciation is most underestimated).
- Because friendships (not kin) decay with reduced shared activity, features should prioritize **shared activities** (not only messages) for friends; kin may tolerate lower frequency.
- "One quality conversation a day" is a well-supported, simple daily goal; the content can be lightweight (catching up, joking), so prompts need not be heavy.
- Maintenance quality (positivity, supportiveness, openness) can be scaffolded via conversation prompts and reminders of friends' life events.

### Gaps
- No precise, empirically derived "minimum contact frequency" to prevent decay was found beyond Dunbar's layer heuristics and Roberts & Dunbar's small sample.
- Did not retrieve evidence on whether app-mediated reminders/nudges themselves improve maintenance outcomes (randomized product trials).

## 6. How do needs differ across groups?

### Takeaway
Loneliness and disconnection are now highest among the young (adolescents and adults under ~30, globally and in the US) and acute for young US men, midlife adults (45–59), remote workers, and new parents; older adults remain at risk mainly through objective isolation.

### Cited Findings

**Young adults / Gen Z / adolescents**
- WHO (2025): highest loneliness among **13–17-year-olds (20.9%)**; adolescent social isolation ~27%. — [Science Media Centre Spain](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/one-six-people-worldwide-suffered-loneliness-last-decade)
- Meta–Gallup (2022–23): ages 19–29 loneliest globally (27%) vs 65+ (17%). — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/512618/almost-quarter-world-feels-lonely.aspx)
- Cigna 2025 (fielded mid-2024): Gen Z 67% lonely vs boomers 44%. — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/many-americans-are-lonely-and-gen-z-most-of-all-study-finds)
- ATUS: 15–24-year-olds' socializing fell from ~60 to ~35 min/day (2003→2025); in-person time with friends fell ~70% (2003→2020). — [Axios, Jul 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/05/americans-socializing-decline); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/loneliness-surgeon-general-epidemic-covid/)
- EdWeek (Aug 2025): teens described as the loneliest age group in WHO's report. — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/teens-are-the-loneliest-people-in-the-world-a-new-report-finds-why/2025/08)

**Men**
- Gallup (May 2025; 2023–24 data): **25%** of US men aged 15–34 felt lonely "a lot of yesterday" vs **18%** US average; OECD median for young men **15%**; US is one of only three OECD countries (with Iceland, Denmark) where young men are lonelier than other adults. — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/690788/younger-men-among-loneliest-west.aspx); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Health_News/2025/05/20/young-men-loneliness/4341747781728/)
- Survey Center (2021): men with **no close friends 3% (1990) → 15% (2021)**; 1 in 5 single men report no close friends. — [Daily Citizen](https://dailycitizen.focusonthefamily.com/survey-finds-americans-increasingly-report-having-no-close-friends-especially-younger-men/); [National Review](https://www.nationalreview.com/2021/07/american-men-suffer-a-friendship-recession/amp/)
- AARP (2025): among 45+, men lonelier than women (**42% vs 37%**), a reversal from rough parity in 2018. — [AARP press release](https://press.aarp.org/2025-12-03-New-AARP-Research-Loneliness-is-on-the-Rise)
- Oswald et al. (2004): men's same-sex friendships involve less supportiveness, openness and interaction than women's — suggesting men may rely more on shared-activity ("side-by-side") contexts. — [ProQuest](https://www.proquest.com/docview/224662281)

**Midlife and older adults**
- AARP (2025): **46%** of ages 45–59 lonely vs **35%** of 60+; lonely adults spend **7.3 hours/day alone** vs 5.6 for all 45+; higher among lower-income, less-educated, unemployed. — [AARP Research](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/relationships/loneliness-social-connections-2025/); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/mental-health/who-are-the-loneliest-americans)
- Steptoe et al. (2013): among older adults, **objective isolation** (not loneliness) independently predicted mortality. — [PMC3625264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625264)

**New parents**
- NCT (UK, Jan 2025, >2,000 new/expectant parents): **62%** feel lonely or isolated at least some of the time; **12%** always. — [Patient Safety Learning hub summary of NCT report](https://www.pslhub.org/learn/patient-safety-in-health-and-care/high-risk-areas/maternity/from-expectation-to-reality-parents%E2%80%99-experiences-of-pregnancy-birth-and-life-with-a-newborn-nct-19-january-2025-r13986/)
- Ohio State Wexner Medical Center (US, 2024, n=265 parents): up to **66%** sometimes/frequently feel isolated and lonely (77% of single parents per secondary report). — [Happiest Baby summary](https://secure.happiestbaby.com/blogs/parents/parenthood-loneliness)
- Harvard MCC (2021, Oct 2020 pandemic poll): **51% of mothers with young children** reported serious loneliness. — [MCC Loneliness in America](https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/reports/loneliness-in-america)
- Loneliness during/after pregnancy associated with postnatal depression in both mothers and fathers. — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/why_parents_are_lonely_and_how_they_can_connect)

**People who relocate**
- Hall's friendship-hours study recruited 355 adults who had moved in the prior 6 months, illustrating that relocation resets networks and new friendships require 40–200+ hours each. — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)
- Roberts & Dunbar (2011): after moving (school → university), friendships decayed faster than kin ties as contact fell. — [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/131226437.pdf)

**Remote workers**
- Gallup *State of the Global Workplace 2024*: daily loneliness **25% fully remote**, **21% hybrid**, **16% fully on-site**; ~20% of all employees globally lonely daily; work location was the variable with the biggest differences. — [Gallup Workplace](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/645566/remote-workers-lonelier.aspx); [ASIS summary](https://www.asisonline.org/security-management-magazine/latest-news/today-in-security/2024/june/one-fifth-of-workers-report-daily-loneliness/)
- Gallup (2025 US): **27%** of fully remote Gen Z employees lonely "a lot of yesterday." — [CPA Practice Advisor, Jul 2025](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2025/07/09/77-of-gen-z-employees-feel-lonely-at-work-new-study-finds/164599/)
- Hall (2018): hours working together contribute little to friendship compared with leisure time — so workplace contact alone may not build friendship even for on-site workers. — [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)

### Inferences
- **Young people** need help with *initiation and unstructured hang-out time* (the ATUS collapse is largest there); **young men** may respond better to activity-based, side-by-side formats; **midlife adults** (most lonely in AARP 2025) are a neglected segment; **new parents** need proximity-based, low-logistics, child-compatible micro-gatherings; **relocators** need structured recurring exposure to rebuild hours; **remote workers** need non-work leisure contact.
- Older adults' mortality risk is driven more by objective isolation, so for them features that increase actual contact frequency (calls, visits) are most defensible.

### Gaps
- No authoritative US national data on loneliness among recent movers/relocators was found.
- New-parent statistics are mostly from UK or sponsored/small US surveys; no large, representative US survey with mother/father splits was found.
- No data found specific to LGBTQ+, disability, or racial/ethnic subgroups (outside this scope's explicit list but relevant for product inclusivity).
